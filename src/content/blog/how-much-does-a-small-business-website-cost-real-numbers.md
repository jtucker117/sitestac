## The short answer

A small business website costs whatever it costs to solve your specific problem — and the honest range is wide. A one-page site for a solo electrician sits at the bottom end. A twelve-page site with online booking, service-area pages, custom photography, and a form that pushes leads into your CRM sits several times higher. Most small businesses land somewhere in the middle, and for a trade doing decent ticket sizes, the whole build usually costs less than a single good job.

That's the useful answer. The rest of this page explains why the range is so wide, what pushes your number up or down, and how to tell a fair quote from a bad one.

## Why nobody will give you a number over the phone

Ask three web companies what a website costs and you'll get three non-answers. It's annoying, but the reason is legitimate.

It's the same reason you don't quote a bathroom remodel over the phone. "How much for a bathroom?" depends on whether you're swapping a vanity or moving drain lines. A website is the same. "Five pages" could mean five pages of text someone hands you on a Word doc, or five pages where somebody has to interview you, write the copy, source photos, and build a quote calculator.

What you *can* do is understand the cost drivers well enough to have a real conversation. Then a quote takes ten minutes instead of three meetings.

## The four ways to buy a website

Before we get into what drives price, it helps to know what you're actually choosing between. Each of these has a completely different cost shape.

### 1. Do it yourself on a website builder

You sign up for a platform, pick a template, and type your own content. This is the cheapest cash outlay by a mile and the most expensive in hours.

It works if you have genuine time, some patience, and a simple business. It falls apart when you're already working ten-hour days. The most common outcome we see: a half-finished site that's been "almost done" for eight months, still has the template's stock photo of a smiling woman in a hard hat, and the phone number in the footer is your old one.

Be honest about your own bandwidth here. A DIY site you never finish costs more than a paid one, because every month it's not live is a month of leads going to the guy who did finish.

### 2. Hire a freelancer

A solo designer or developer. Usually the middle of the road on price and the widest swing on quality.

Good freelancers are excellent value. The risk is availability. When they take a full-time job, go on holiday, or just stop replying, your website is a locked box. Ask up front: who has admin access, where is the site hosted, and what happens if you're unreachable for two weeks?

### 3. Hire an agency

Highest upfront cost, most process. You get a team — designer, developer, copywriter, project manager — and usually a more polished result with a strategy behind it.

Where agencies get expensive is scope. Discovery workshops, brand guidelines, three rounds of design concepts. That's genuinely valuable for a business with a marketing budget and a growth plan. For a two-truck plumbing outfit that just needs to stop losing calls to the competition, a lot of it is overhead you're paying for.

### 4. A managed or subscription build

Someone builds it, hosts it, maintains it, and makes your edits — you pay monthly instead of one big cheque. Lower barrier to entry, predictable cost, and nobody's chasing you for a deposit before work starts.

The thing to check is ownership. Some subscription setups mean you never own the site and lose everything if you leave. Others mean you own the content and domain and can walk with them. Ask directly. A straight answer is a good sign.

## What actually drives your price up or down

Here's where the number comes from. Almost every quote you'll ever get is some combination of these.

**Page count.** The obvious one, but not in the way people think. Ten pages of the same layout with different text is barely more work than five. Ten pages that each need their own design is a different job entirely.

**Who writes the words.** This is the single biggest hidden cost driver. If you supply finished copy, you save real money. If someone has to interview you, understand your services, and write it — that's a skilled job that takes hours per page. Most business owners *say* they'll write it themselves. Most don't. Be realistic and price it in.

**Photography.** Stock photos are cheap and they look like stock photos. Real photos of your crew, your vans, your finished work, and your shop convert dramatically better. If you already have a decent phone-photo library from past jobs, you've saved yourself a chunk. If you need a photographer on site — or short video for the site and Google profile — that's a line item, and bundling it with the website usually costs less than hiring separately later. Same goes for matching [print design](/blog/custom-print-design-business-cards-yard-signs/) (cards, yard signs, trifolds) so offline materials don't look like a different company.

**Custom design vs. template.** A good template, adapted properly to your brand, gets most small businesses 90% of the way there for a fraction of the cost. Fully custom design is where budgets balloon. Unless you're in a business where the look *is* the product — a high-end restaurant, a designer salon, a luxury remodeler — a well-executed template is usually the smarter buy.

**Functionality.** A brochure site that says who you are and how to reach you is the baseline. Every function you add on top costs more:

- Contact form that emails you: baseline, always included.
- Quote request form with photo upload: a bit more.
- Online booking with real-time availability: meaningfully more.
- Payments or deposits taken online: more again.
- Full e-commerce with inventory: a different category of project.
- Customer portal or login area: the top end.

**Integrations.** Pushing leads into your CRM, syncing a booking calendar with your scheduling software, connecting a POS, wiring up call tracking. Each one is a small project. Two or three of them add up fast.

**Service area and location pages.** A single-location salon needs one location page. A HVAC company covering fifteen towns and wanting to rank in each one needs a strategy — and fifteen pages that don't read like the same page copy-pasted fifteen times. This is legitimate work and it's worth paying for if local search is your lead source.

**Migration.** Moving an existing site — especially an older one with years of blog posts and rankings — takes more care than starting fresh. Redirects have to be mapped properly or you lose traffic you spent years earning.

**Revision rounds.** Most quotes include a set number. If you're a committee of four with strong opinions, ask what happens after the included rounds. Endless revisions are how a fixed-price job turns into a fight.

## What should be included in any quote

Ask for these in writing. If a quote doesn't mention them, ask why.

- **Mobile-responsive design.** Most of your traffic is on a phone. This is not an upgrade in the current era; it's the floor.
- **A domain, connected properly, registered in your name.** Yours. Not theirs.
- **Hosting and SSL.** The padlock in the browser bar. Sites without it get flagged as insecure.
- **Basic on-page SEO.** Page titles, descriptions, heading structure, image alt text, a sitemap submitted to Google. This isn't an SEO campaign — it's basic hygiene and it should be baked in.
- **Google Business Profile connection.** For a local business this drives more calls than the website itself in a lot of cases.
- **Analytics.** So you can see what's actually happening.
- **Contact forms that work and get tested.** You'd be amazed how many live sites have a form that quietly emails nobody.
- **Page speed that isn't embarrassing.** Slow sites lose people before they load.
- **Training or a handover.** How to log in, how to change your hours, who to call.

## The ongoing costs nobody mentions until later

The build is the visible cost. Running the thing is the one that surprises people.

**Domain renewal.** Annual, small, and easy to forget until your site goes dark. Set it to auto-renew and make sure the card on file isn't expired.

**Hosting.** Monthly or annual. Cheap shared hosting is cheap for a reason — slow load times and support that takes days.

**Software and licence renewals.** If your site runs on WordPress with premium plugins, those plugins renew. Booking systems, form tools, and page builders often have their own subscriptions.

**Maintenance and updates.** Platforms and plugins update constantly, mostly for security. A site left untouched for two years is a site waiting to get defaced or start throwing errors. This is either your job, someone's retainer, or included in a managed plan.

**Backups.** Should be automatic and off-site. Cheap insurance.

**Content edits.** New service, new price list, new team member, seasonal hours. Either you make these yourself or you pay someone. Ask how edits are handled and how fast — "we'll get to it within a week" is a problem when a storm hits and you want an emergency banner up today.

**Marketing.** SEO work, Google Ads, review generation. Separate from the website, but a website with no traffic strategy is a business card in a drawer.

When you're comparing quotes, add up two years of running costs alongside the build price. The cheap build with expensive hosting and per-edit charges often loses.

## Upfront vs. monthly: which makes sense for you

Paying once, upfront, means you own it outright and your ongoing costs are just hosting and maintenance. It's usually the cheaper path over a long horizon — if you actually keep the site maintained.

Paying monthly spreads the cost, bundles hosting and support, and means someone's responsible when something breaks. For a business where cash flow matters more than total cost — which is most small businesses — that predictability is worth a lot.

Neither is wrong. What's wrong is a monthly deal where you're locked in for years and don't own your own domain.

## Red flags that should make you slow down

**A quote with no scope.** A number with no detail behind it isn't a quote, it's a guess. It will change.

**No admin access for you.** If you can't log in, you don't control your website.

**The domain registered to them.** This is the single worst position to be in. Your domain is your business address. Never let someone else own it.

**"SEO included" with no explanation.** Basic on-page SEO should be included. A ranking guarantee shouldn't exist, because nobody controls Google.

**Suspiciously cheap.** Somebody is either using a template with your logo dropped in and calling it done, or they're going to make it back on hosting and change fees. Sometimes both.

**No written timeline.** "A few weeks" turns into five months. Get milestones.

**Won't say what happens if you leave.** Ask: can I take my site, my content, and my domain with me? A good provider answers immediately.

## Think about it as a lead cost, not a bill

Here's the frame that makes the whole decision easier.

Work out what one customer is worth to you. A roofer's average job is a big number. A dentist's new patient is worth a lot over a few years. A salon client on a six-week cycle adds up. Even a lunch spot's regular is worth real money annually.

Now ask how many extra customers the website needs to bring in over a year to pay for itself. For most trades, it's one. Sometimes it's a fraction of one. A landscaper who books two additional installs off the site has covered the build and the hosting and the maintenance and is ahead.

That's the actual math. Not "is this expensive" but "how many jobs does this need to produce." When you look at it that way, the risk isn't overspending — it's buying something so cheap it produces nothing.

The flip side is real too. Don't buy a custom-designed, fully integrated, e-commerce-enabled platform when what you needed was four clean pages, good photos, and a phone number people can tap. Overbuying is just as common as underbuying.

## Roughly what to expect by business type

No numbers, but here's the relative ladder:

**Solo trade or single-service business.** A handful of pages, clear services, strong calls to action, Google Business Profile connected. Bottom of the range. Fast to build.

**Multi-service local business — plumbing, HVAC, electrical, landscaping.** Service pages, service-area pages, gallery, reviews, quote form. Middle of the range. This is the most common small business build and the one with the best return.

**Appointment businesses — salons, clinics, studios, detailers.** Everything above plus real-time booking and reminders. Middle to upper, depending on how deep the booking integration goes.

**Restaurants.** Menus that are easy to update, ordering or reservation integration, photos that make people hungry. Middle, unless you're doing full online ordering in-house.

**Retail or product sales.** E-commerce is its own category. Product catalogues, payments, shipping rules, inventory. Top of the range and ongoing work forever.

**Multi-location or franchise.** Location pages, separate contact routing, sometimes separate branding. Top of the range because complexity scales with locations.

## How to get an accurate quote fast

Send this to anyone you're asking. You'll get sharper numbers and fewer meetings.

1. What you do and where you do it.
2. Your service list.
3. Do you have an existing site, and what's wrong with it.
4. Do you have logo, brand colours, and photos, or do you need them.
5. Will you write the copy or do you need it written.
6. Do you need booking, payments, or a shop.
7. What software you use that the site needs to talk to.
8. Two or three websites you like, and why.
9. When you need it live.
10. Who makes the final decision.

That's it. Any decent provider can price a job off that list.

## One last thing

The most expensive website is the one that doesn't bring you work. That's true of a cheap one that looks broken on a phone, and it's true of an expensive one that took nine months and nobody can find on Google.

Spend enough to get something that loads fast, looks like a real business, and makes it obvious how to contact you. Everything beyond that is optional and can be added later.
