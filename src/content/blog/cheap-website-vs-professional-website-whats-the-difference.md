## The short answer

A cheap website is a page that exists. A professional website is a tool built to get you phone calls. Both can look fine in a screenshot — the difference shows up in how fast it loads, whether it shows up in search, whether it's set up to turn a visitor into a booked job, and whether anyone answers when it breaks at 7am on a Saturday.

That's it. The rest of this post is the detail behind that.

## "Cheap" usually means one of three things

When an owner tells me they got a cheap site, it's almost always one of these:

**A DIY template.** You picked a theme, dropped in your logo, and typed some text. It's yours, you own it, it cost almost nothing but a few weekends. It works right up until you need it to do something specific.

**A guy who does sites on the side.** Nephew, a friend's kid, somebody off Facebook. Fast and inexpensive. The risk isn't the build — it's six months later when you need a change and he's stopped answering.

**An overseas template shop.** You filled out a form, they sent back a site in a week. Looks clean. Usually generic, usually slow, and usually impossible to get edited without starting a ticket.

None of those are scams. Plenty of businesses run on them for years. But you should know what you didn't pay for.

## What you actually didn't pay for

### Someone deciding what goes where

A professional build starts with questions: What jobs do you want more of? What's your service area? What do people call you about? Then the site gets laid out around the answers.

A cheap build starts with a template, and your business gets poured into whatever shape the template already was. That's why so many contractor sites open with a giant photo and the word "Welcome" instead of the three towns you serve and a phone number.

Real example: a plumber we worked with had a nice-looking template site. The phone number was in the footer. On mobile, that meant scrolling past nine screens of content to find it. We moved it to a sticky bar at the top. Same site, same traffic, more calls.

### Speed

Cheap sites are usually heavy. Full-size photos straight off a phone, four plugins doing the job of one, a slideshow nobody asked for. On a good wifi connection it feels fine. On a phone in a truck with two bars, it's four seconds of white screen — and most people are gone before it finishes.

This is the single most common thing we find when we audit a DIY site, and it's invisible from the owner's desk.

### Local search setup

Showing up when someone searches "electrician near me" isn't magic, but it is work. Page titles that say what you do and where. Your name, address and phone matching exactly what's on your Google listing. Separate pages for separate services instead of one page listing everything. Schema markup so Google knows you're a business and not a blog.

Template builders don't do any of that for you. They can't — they don't know what town you're in.

### The stuff that turns a visitor into a job

Click-to-call that actually works on mobile. A form that emails you instantly instead of sitting in a dashboard you never log into. Photos of your own work, not stock. Reviews on the page, not just on Google. A clear next step on every page.

That's the gap between a brochure and a sales tool, and it's mostly invisible until you compare two months of call volume.

### Someone to call

This is the one that bites people. SSL certificate expires and the site throws a security warning. A plugin update breaks the contact form. Hosting account lapses because the card on file expired. Site goes down the week you're running a mailer.

With a professional setup, that's somebody's job. With a cheap one, it's yours, at 10pm, Googling error messages.

## Where cheap is genuinely fine

I'm not going to tell you everyone needs a custom build.

If you're brand new and not sure the business will stick, a simple one-pager with your phone number and service area is better than nothing and better than waiting six months for perfect. If you get 100% of your work from one builder or one referral source and the website is just there so people can confirm you're real, a basic site does that job.

The moment it stops being fine is when you start *depending* on the site for work. Once you're spending money on ads, or you're trying to grow into a new town, or you want to stop relying on one referral source — now the site is load-bearing, and cheap starts costing you.

## How to tell which one you've got right now

Five-minute check, do it on your phone:

1. **Load your homepage on cellular, not wifi.** Count the seconds. Over three, you have a speed problem.
2. **Try to call yourself.** How many taps from landing on the homepage? Should be one.
3. **Search your service plus your town.** Are you anywhere? Page one, page three, nowhere?
4. **Submit your own contact form.** Did the email arrive? You'd be amazed how often it doesn't.
5. **Ask yourself who fixes it.** If the answer is "I'd have to find someone," that's the real difference.

Fail two or more and it's probably worth a conversation.

## The honest summary

Cheap buys you a presence. Professional buys you a lead source. Neither is wrong — it depends entirely on whether you need people to find you, or just to believe you exist once they've already got your number.
