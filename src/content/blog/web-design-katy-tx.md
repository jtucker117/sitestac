Good web design in Katy, TX comes down to two things most sites get wrong: being clear about exactly which parts of the Katy area you cover, and being fast and obvious enough that a homeowner who just got your name from a neighbor can confirm you're legit in about fifteen seconds. Everything else — colors, animations, clever taglines — is decoration on top of those two jobs.

Here's why that plays out differently in Katy than it does closer in to Houston.

## "Katy" covers a lot more ground than the city of Katy

The actual city limits are small. The Katy mailing address sprawls way past them, across three counties, through a long run of master-planned neighborhoods on both sides of I-10. Somebody in a subdivision ten minutes from the city line will tell you flat out that they live in Katy, and they're not wrong in any way that matters to them.

That creates a real problem for your website. A customer searching "fence repair Katy" and a customer searching from a neighborhood that's technically unincorporated Fort Bend County are both looking for you, but Google is sorting results partly by physical proximity to where that person is standing. If your shop is on the east side and the lead is way out west past the Grand Parkway, you may not show up on the map at all.

Your site is how you fix that. Not with a list of fifty ZIP codes crammed into the footer — that trick stopped working years ago. With actual pages, or at least real sections, that name the areas you work in and say something true about working there. A foundation repair company can talk about what the soil does in the newer sections versus the older parts of town. An HVAC company can note that half the homes out there are under fifteen years old and still on original builder-grade equipment. That's content a Houston competitor isn't going to bother writing.

## Most of your leads already know your name

Katy runs on neighborhood groups. Somebody posts asking for a pool guy, a dozen neighbors name names, and the person who asked then opens Google and types that name in. Half the time they don't even remember whether it was a company or a guy — they just type what they remember.

So the first question your website answers isn't "why should I hire you." It's "is this the one they were talking about, and are they real."

That changes what belongs at the top of the page. Your business name spelled the way people say it. A phone number you can tap. A photo of an actual truck or an actual crew — not a stock image of a smiling man in a hard hat holding a clipboard. Reviews visible without scrolling. The areas you serve. If somebody has to hunt for any of that, they go back to the group and try the second name on the list.

This is the same reason specific, trade-focused pages outperform generic ones. ## You're competing with Houston companies, not just Katy ones

This is the part local owners underestimate. The big west-side Houston operations — the ones with a marketing department and a budget — treat Katy as a target market. They run ads into it, they build pages for it, and they do not care that their office is twenty-five minutes east.

You cannot outspend them. You can out-specific them.

A big company's "Katy" page is usually a template with the city name swapped in. Yours can mention that you've worked in these neighborhoods for years, that you know which builders put in what, that you know how long the drive is from one side of the Grand Parkway to the other on a weekday afternoon. It can show jobs you actually did. It can have your face on it.

Homeowners in Katy read that difference instantly. A lot of them moved here from somewhere else and are actively trying to find local people instead of a call center. Give them an easy reason to pick you.

## Build for a phone held in a kitchen

Nearly all of this traffic is mobile. A homeowner standing in the kitchen with a leak, or sitting on the couch after the kids go down, scrolling with one thumb.

What that means practically:

- **Tap-to-call button that's always reachable.** Not buried under a menu.
- **Pages that load before they lose patience.** Slow usually means oversized photos or a bloated theme stacked with plugins nobody uses.
- **Short forms.** Name, phone, what's wrong, where. Asking for a budget range or a preferred contact window on the first form loses people.
- **Readable without pinching.** Sounds obvious. Go check your own site on your own phone right now.

If you're a restaurant, salon, or studio in Katy instead of a trade, the same rules hold with different buttons — menu, hours, and directions up front, booking one tap away. Nobody wants a PDF menu.

## What actually drives the cost

No two of these jobs price the same, so here's what moves the number instead.

**Pushes it up:** a lot of separate service pages, online booking or scheduling tied to software you already run, e-commerce, menus or galleries that change often, multiple locations, or starting from nothing on photography and written content.

**Keeps it down:** a tight set of pages built well, photos you already have from jobs, and knowing what you want to say before anyone starts building.

**Usually included and worth confirming:** hosting, an SSL certificate, mobile layout, basic on-page SEO, and your Google Business Profile connected properly. Ask directly who owns the domain and the site files when it's done. The answer should be you.

The ongoing piece matters more than people expect. A site that never gets touched slides down the rankings while competitors keep adding pages and collecting reviews. ## The things that move the needle after launch

A website alone doesn't rank. In this market, three things do most of the work:

1. **Google Business Profile, filled out completely** — correct category, real service area, photos added regularly, questions answered.
2. **Reviews, steadily.** Katy homeowners read them. Ten recent reviews beat forty from four years ago.
3. **Pages that match how people search.** "Emergency AC repair Katy" is a different page than "AC installation." Same company, different intent, different page.

That combination is also what keeps you visible when a storm rolls through and demand spikes overnight. The companies that get called in that week are the ones already showing up before it happened.
