## The short answer

Yes — if people ever look you up before they call you, you need a website. That's almost every local business now, including the ones that get all their work by word of mouth, because the first thing someone does after a friend says "call this guy" is type your name into Google. A website is the page that answers their questions when you're on a roof, under a sink, or behind a chair with your hands full.

The honest exception: if you're fully booked for the next year, turning work away, and don't plan to grow or sell the business, you can get by without one for a while. Most owners who say that are one slow quarter away from changing their mind.

Now the longer version.

## What actually happens when someone hears your name

Picture a homeowner whose water heater just died. Her neighbor says, "Use Dave, he did ours." She doesn't call Dave right then. She types "Dave's Plumbing" into her phone while she's standing in a wet basement.

Three things can happen:

1. **She finds a website.** It says Dave does water heaters, lists the towns he covers, shows a photo of his truck, has a phone number she can tap. She calls. Total elapsed time: forty seconds.
2. **She finds a Facebook page.** Last post was eight months ago, a photo of a job with no caption. Hours say "always open," which she doesn't believe. She hesitates.
3. **She finds nothing.** Or she finds three other Daves in neighboring counties. She goes back to Google, searches "water heater replacement near me," and calls whoever shows up first.

That third outcome is the one that costs you money, and you never find out it happened. Nobody calls to tell you they couldn't find you.

That's the real job of a small business website. It's not a billboard. It's the thing that catches the referrals you already earned.

## "But all my work comes from word of mouth"

Good. That means your work is good. It doesn't mean you don't need a website — it means you need one more than the guy who buys leads.

Word of mouth has a leak in it. Someone recommends you at a barbecue, the other person forgets your name by Tuesday, and that's the end of it. A website plugs that leak. It turns "some electrician my brother-in-law used" into a searchable, callable business.

It also does a few things a referral can't:

- **It pre-qualifies people.** If your site says you don't do mobile homes, or you have a minimum service call, or you only cover three counties, you stop getting calls you were going to turn down anyway. That's an hour a week back.
- **It sells the jobs you want.** A roofer who mostly gets patch calls can put gutter and full-replacement work front and center. People call for what they see.
- **It backs up the referral.** Your buddy says you're great. Your site shows twelve photos of finished decks and a license number. Now they believe him.

## What about my Google Business Profile?

Your Google Business Profile — the box with your reviews, hours, and map pin — is genuinely important. For a lot of local trades it brings in more calls than anything else. Set it up, verify it, keep it current, collect reviews.

But it's not a replacement for a website, for two reasons.

**First, Google itself wants to see one.** A profile linked to a real website with real content about your services and service area tends to perform better than a profile with nothing behind it. You're giving Google more to work with.

**Second, it's rented.** Google owns that listing. It can be suspended for a reason you don't understand, a competitor can report it, your layout can change overnight. People who've been locked out of their profile for three weeks learn fast what it feels like to have no address of your own.

Same goes for Facebook and Instagram. Great for showing work and staying in front of past customers. Terrible as your only presence. You don't control the reach, the rules, or whether the account exists next year.

The pattern that works: Google Business Profile for discovery, social for staying visible, website as home base that you own.

## When you probably don't need one (yet)

I'd rather be straight with you than sell you something you can't use.

You can likely skip a website for now if:

- **You're a one-person operation at capacity and intend to stay there.** A solo tile setter booked out eight months with no plan to hire doesn't urgently need a site. Though you might still want one for the day a big customer dries up.
- **You're subcontracting exclusively.** If 100% of your work comes from two general contractors, your website isn't what gets you paid. A clean one-pager with your license and insurance info is plenty.
- **You're pre-revenue and testing.** If you're not sure the business will exist in six months, don't start with a website. Start with a Google Business Profile and a phone number and go get customers.

Everybody else — if you're trying to grow, hire, raise your rates, or ever sell the business — the question isn't whether to have one. It's what kind.

## What different businesses actually need

A "website" means wildly different things depending on what you do. Here's what it looks like in practice.

### Trades and home services

Plumber, electrician, HVAC, roofer, landscaper, painter. Your site needs to do four things:

- Say exactly what you do, in the words customers use ("drain cleaning," not "hydro-jetting solutions")
- List the towns and neighborhoods you serve
- Show your work — real photos from your phone beat stock photos every time
- Make it dead easy to call or text from a phone

Emergency trades need one extra thing: the phone number big, at the top, tappable, with your hours or "24/7" right next to it. Someone with water coming through the ceiling isn't scrolling.

### Restaurants and food

Menu, hours, address, parking situation, and whether you take reservations. That's most of it. The number one reason people visit a restaurant's site is to check the menu and the hours, and the number one reason they leave annoyed is a menu that's a PDF they have to pinch-zoom on a phone.

If you do catering or private events, that gets its own page. Those are your highest-margin jobs and they don't come from the walk-in crowd.

### Salons, barbers, spas

Services with some idea of what's involved, your stylists, a gallery, and online booking. Booking is the big one. A huge chunk of people would rather book at 10pm on their phone than call during the day. If your site can't take a booking, you're losing those.

### Professional services

Accountant, lawyer, consultant, bookkeeper. People are buying judgment, so the site is about trust. Who you are, who you help, credentials, a few specifics about the kinds of problems you solve. Short and confident beats long and vague.

### Retail and shops

Hours, location, what you carry, and whether it's worth the drive. You don't need to sell online to benefit from a website — most local shops get more value from "yes, we have that in stock, come by" than from building a full store.

## What a website actually costs — and what moves the number

I'm not going to throw a figure at you, because the honest answer is that it depends entirely on what you need, and any number on a public page turns into a promise somebody holds you to.

What I can tell you is what drives the cost.

**Things that keep it low:**

- A small number of pages. Five good pages beat twenty thin ones.
- Content you provide. If you can write a paragraph about each service and send photos from your phone, that's real money saved.
- No custom functionality. A site that informs and takes calls is simpler than one that processes payments.
- A template or proven layout instead of a design built from scratch.

**Things that push it up:**

- Online booking or scheduling tied to your calendar
- Taking payments or deposits
- Lots of individual service pages or location pages (worth it if you serve a wide area, but it's more work)
- Professional photography and video
- Copywriting from scratch when you don't have time to write anything
- Migrating from an old site without breaking your existing Google rankings

Then there's the ongoing piece, which people forget. Hosting, your domain name, security updates, backups, and someone to fix it when something breaks. A website isn't a one-time purchase, it's more like a truck — you buy it once and maintain it forever. Any quote that doesn't mention ongoing costs is leaving something out.

The cheapest website is the one you build yourself on a drag-and-drop builder. That's a real option. The cost is your time, and the risk is that you get it 70% done and leave it that way for two years. I've seen more half-finished DIY sites than finished ones.

## The pages you actually need

Most small business sites are overbuilt. Here's the lean version that works:

**Home.** What you do, where you do it, and how to reach you — all visible without scrolling. Then proof: photos, reviews, years in business.

**Services.** Either one page listing everything, or separate pages for your main money-makers. Separate pages are better for search, because someone typing "gutter cleaning [your town]" is going to land on a page about gutter cleaning, not a general list.

**About.** People hire people. A photo of you and your crew, how long you've been doing this, why you started. Skip the mission statement.

**Service area.** Name the towns. Literally list them. This is how you stop getting calls from an hour away and start showing up for the places you want to work.

**Contact.** Phone, email, a short form, hours. If you have a shop, the address and a map.

That's it. You can add a gallery, reviews page, FAQ, or blog later. Don't let a wish list stop you from getting the basic five live.

## Signs the site you already have is working against you

A bad website can be worse than no website, because it actively tells people you're not current. Red flags:

- **It doesn't work on a phone.** Most of your visitors are on a phone. If they have to zoom and drag, they're gone.
- **It's slow.** More than a few seconds to load and people bail. Usually it's enormous uncompressed photos.
- **The information is wrong.** Old hours, a disconnected number, a service you stopped offering. This one actually costs you jobs.
- **No phone number at the top.** Shocking how common this is.
- **A "copyright 2017" in the footer.** Small thing, but people notice. It reads as "out of business."
- **Browser warns it's not secure.** Missing security certificate. Easy fix, bad look.

If you've got a site that ticks several of those, you don't necessarily need to start from scratch. Sometimes it's a cleanup job.

## How to tell if it's doing anything

You don't need a dashboard full of charts. Two questions:

**Are you getting calls or form submissions you wouldn't have gotten otherwise?** Easiest way to find out: ask. "How'd you hear about us?" takes four seconds and tells you more than most analytics.

**Can you find yourself on Google?** Search your business name. Then search what you do plus your town. Do it in an incognito window so your own history doesn't skew it. If you're invisible on the second search, that's the gap to work on — and that's a longer game involving your Google Business Profile, reviews, and service pages that actually talk about your town.

A website for a local business isn't usually a flood of new leads. It's a steady background effect: more of your referrals convert, fewer tire-kickers waste your time, and you look like a real company when a bigger customer checks you out.

## The part nobody mentions

If you ever want to sell your business, hand it to your kid, or bring on a partner, the website is part of the asset. A business with a real web presence, a domain it owns, reviews tied to a real profile, and a few years of history is worth more than a guy with a phone number. That's not a marketing argument, that's an accounting one.

Same with hiring. Good techs look you up before they apply. A real website signals a business that's going somewhere.

## So — do you need one?

If people search your name, yes. If you want work from outside your existing circle, yes. If you want to control your own presence instead of renting it from a platform, yes.

Start small. Five pages, honest photos, your phone number big at the top, and the towns you serve spelled out. You can add to it forever, but you can't get back the calls you missed last month.
