## The short answer

A small business website in Katy costs what it costs because of three things: how many pages you actually need, whether anything on the site has to *do* something (booking, quoting, online ordering, payments), and how hard your category is to rank in. A four-page site for a solo electrician who works Cinco Ranch and Firethorne is a fraction of the work of a 20-page site for a remodeler who wants to show up for a dozen service terms across Harris, Fort Bend, and Waller counties. Nobody can quote you honestly without knowing which one you are.

Below is what moves that number up or down, specific to doing business in Katy.

## Katy is a harder market than its size suggests

Katy is not a small town with a few competitors. It's a sprawl of master-planned neighborhoods along I-10 and the Grand Parkway, filled with newer homes owned by people who are comfortable searching, comparing, and reading reviews before they call anybody. A lot of those homeowners commute into the Energy Corridor or downtown and make the call from a phone at lunch.

That means two things for your website budget.

First, you're not only competing with other Katy businesses. You're competing with Houston-wide companies that list Katy as a service area and spend real money to show up there. A roofing or HVAC company with 40 trucks will outspend you on ads every day of the week. What you can beat them on is being *actually from here* — and your website is where that either comes across or doesn't.

Second, Katy addresses are messy. A huge share of "Katy, TX" mailing addresses sit outside the city limits, in unincorporated Harris County or over in Fort Bend. Your customer in one subdivision and your customer three miles down the road may not think of themselves as being in the same place at all. A site that just says "Serving Katy" leaves money on the table. A site that names the areas you actually cover does better — and that's more pages, which is a real cost driver.

## The four things that move the price

**Page count.** This is the biggest one and the most predictable. One page per service you want to be found for, plus the areas you cover, plus the basics — about, contact, reviews. A pressure washing guy might need six. A general contractor who does kitchens, baths, additions, and outdoor living needs a lot more.

**Functionality.** A phone number and a contact form is simple. Online booking that syncs to your calendar, a quote form that branches based on what the customer picks, online ordering for a restaurant, deposits taken through the site — each of those adds build time. Be honest about whether you'll actually use them. Plenty of Katy trades pay for a booking system and then keep answering the phone anyway.

**Content.** Who's writing the words and where are the photos coming from? If you hand over a folder of job photos and can talk through your services on a call, that's cheap. If someone has to come shoot photos and write everything from scratch, that's a line item. Original photos of your own crews and your own finished work are worth paying for in Katy specifically — this market has seen enough stock photos of smiling fake plumbers to tune them out.

**Competition in your category.** Lawn care, pool service, HVAC, and roofing are crowded here. Showing up for those takes more than a pretty site — it takes structured service pages, a well-built Google Business Profile, and consistency over months. A niche trade with four competitors in the area needs far less. ## Where Katy owners overspend

**Service-area pages they can't back up.** Someone will sell you 30 location pages — Katy, Fulshear, Brookshire, Richmond, Cypress, and on and on — all saying the same thing with the city name swapped. Google figured that out years ago. Those pages don't rank, and you paid for all of them. Build pages for the areas you genuinely work and can show a job from.

**Design that nobody sees.** Animations, parallax scrolling, video headers. Most of your traffic is a homeowner on a phone, outside, with one bar, wanting your number. Fancy build, slow load, lost call.

**Monthly plans with nothing in them.** Some shops charge an ongoing fee that covers hosting and essentially nothing else. Hosting is not expensive. If you're paying monthly, ask exactly what work happens in a given month.

## Where cutting costs backfires

The cheapest site is one you build yourself on a template over a few weekends. For some businesses that's genuinely fine. Where it falls apart is speed, mobile layout, and the structure that search engines use to figure out what you do and where. We walked through the technical side of that in [Plumber Website Design in Sugar Land: What Wins Jobs](/blog/plumber-website-design-sugar-land/) — the same build problems show up in every trade.

The other common mistake is skipping the Google Business Profile work. In Katy, the map pack is where a lot of the calls come from. A beautiful website with a neglected profile is a storefront on a road with no sign.

## What's usually included, and what isn't

Most reputable builds include design, the pages agreed up front, mobile layout, contact forms, basic on-page search setup, and connecting your Google Business Profile. Typically *not* included: ongoing ad management, writing new content every month, professional photo and video shoots, and custom software. Get that split in writing before you start.

Also ask who owns the site. If you leave, do you take it with you? That answer matters more than the price.

## How to compare two quotes fairly

Line up page count, functionality, who writes the content, what happens after launch, and whether ongoing cost buys actual work. Nine times out of ten, the "expensive" quote is simply the one that spelled out more.
