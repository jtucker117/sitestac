## The short answer

Website design pricing for a small business comes down to three things: how many pages you need, how much custom work goes into the design, and who's writing the words and taking the photos. A five-page site for a plumber built on a proven layout sits at the low end. A restaurant with online ordering, a reservation system, and a photographer on site sits much higher.

Most quotes you'll get are built the same way underneath, even when the totals look wildly different. Once you know what's in the stack, comparing them stops being guesswork. (If you want ballpark ranges by business type, we broke those down in [How Much Does a Website Cost for a Small Business?](/blog/how-much-does-a-small-business-website-cost-real-numbers/).)

## What you're actually paying for

A website quote isn't one thing. It's usually five or six line items bundled into a single number. Here's what sits underneath:

**Strategy and structure.** Somebody has to decide what pages you need, what order they go in, and what the visitor is supposed to do when they land. For a roofing company that's usually home, services, service area, gallery, reviews, contact. Skip this step and you get a pretty site nobody calls from.

**Design.** This is the visual layer — colors, fonts, layout, how it looks on a phone. It can mean a template lightly adjusted, or a layout drawn from scratch for your business. Both are legitimate. They cost very different amounts.

**Build.** Turning the design into a working site that loads fast, works on every phone, and doesn't break when you add a page later.

**Content.** Words and pictures. This is the single biggest reason quotes differ, and the one owners always underestimate. More on that below.

**Setup and launch.** Domain, hosting, SSL, Google Business Profile connection, contact forms that actually deliver, analytics so you can see what's happening.

**Ongoing.** Hosting, updates, backups, security. Every site has this whether it's in the quote or not.

If a quote is one number with no breakdown, ask for the breakdown. A shop that can't itemize it either hasn't thought it through or doesn't want you comparing.

## The things that actually push the price up

### Page count — but not the way you think

Adding a page that reuses an existing layout is cheap. Adding a page that needs its own design is not. Ten service pages built off one template is a small job. Ten pages that each look different is a big one.

So when someone asks "how many pages do you need?", the useful follow-up is "how many *different kinds* of pages?"

### Who writes the copy

This is the fork in the road. If you write the words yourself, the price drops and the timeline stretches — because most owners mean to write it, then get busy running a business, and the site sits half-finished for three months.

If the agency writes it, you pay for that time, but the site ships. For a lot of trades, paying someone to write it is the difference between having a website and having a project.

### Photos

Stock photos of a generic kitchen remodel do not sell your kitchen remodels. Real photos of your crew, your trucks, and your finished work convert far better — and someone has to take them. A photographer on site for half a day is a real line item, but for a salon, a restaurant, or a landscaper, it's often the highest-return money in the whole project.

If budget is tight, good phone photos from a job site beat stock every time.

### Functionality beyond "call us"

A contact form is standard. These are not:

- Online booking that syncs with your calendar
- Online ordering or a menu that updates in real time
- E-commerce, even a small store
- Customer login areas or quote portals
- Multi-location pages with separate hours and maps

Each one is real development work plus ongoing maintenance. Be honest with yourself about whether you'll use it. Plenty of HVAC companies pay for booking software and then keep answering the phone anyway.

### Local SEO groundwork

Some shops include the basics — proper page titles, location pages, Google Business Profile setup, schema markup so search engines understand what you do. Others charge for it separately or skip it. This matters more than the design for most local businesses, so find out which camp your quote is in.

## Why two quotes for "the same site" can be miles apart

Usually one of these is happening:

**Different content assumptions.** One quote assumes you supply everything. The other includes writing and photography. Same site, very different work.

**Different depth of design.** Template swap versus custom layout.

**Different definitions of done.** Does "launch" include getting your Google listing pointed at the new site, setting up call tracking, and making sure the old URLs redirect? If not, you'll pay for it later.

**Different ongoing costs.** A low build price with a fat monthly fee can cost more over three years than a higher build with cheap hosting. Do the three-year math.

**Offshore versus local.** There's good work at both ends. The difference tends to show up in communication, revisions, and what happens when you need a change in a hurry.

## What to ask before you sign

- Who owns the site and the domain when it's done? (Answer should be: you.)
- Can I edit text and add pages myself without calling you?
- What's included in ongoing monthly, and what's extra?
- How many rounds of revisions before it becomes a change order?
- What happens if I want to leave — can I take the site with me?
- Is the copy written for my service area, or is it generic?

That last one catches a lot of people. A site that says "we provide quality service" with no mention of the towns you actually work in won't show up when someone three streets over searches for your trade.

## A reasonable way to budget

Don't start from a number. Start from what the site has to do.

If you need the phone to ring for emergency calls, you need a fast site, a clickable phone number at the top, service pages for what you actually do, and real reviews. That's an achievable project.

If you need to stop answering booking calls at 9pm, that's a bigger build, and it should pay for itself in time saved.

Work out how much one new customer is worth to you over a year. Then look at the quote again. For most trades, a site that brings in a handful of jobs has already paid for itself — which is a more useful frame than whether the number feels big.

And whatever you spend, spend it once and spend it right. Rebuilding a bad site costs more than building a good one, because you pay twice and lose the months in between.
