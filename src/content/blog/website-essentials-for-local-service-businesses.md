## The short answer

A local service business website needs six things: what you do, where you do it, proof you've done it before, a phone number that's easy to tap, a form that actually reaches you, and pages that load fast on a phone. Everything else is a nice-to-have. If you nail those six, you'll out-perform most of your competitors, because most of them are missing at least two.

That's the whole answer. Below is the longer version — what each of those pieces looks like in practice, the mistakes that cost you calls, and what to do first if you're starting from nothing.

## Why a service business site is different from a normal website

When someone lands on a plumber's website, they are not browsing. They have water coming through a ceiling, or a boiler that's been making a noise for a week, and they want to know two things fast: *do you handle this, and can you come to me.*

That's a different job than a website for a clothing brand or a software company. You're not building awareness. You're answering a question for someone who is already halfway to hiring somebody. Your only real competition is the two or three other companies they opened in other tabs.

So every decision on your site should be judged against one test: **does this help a ready-to-buy customer decide to call me instead of the other tab?**

Most of the stuff web designers get excited about fails that test. Big hero videos, animated scrolling, a blog nobody reads. Meanwhile the phone number is buried in the footer in grey text.

## Essential #1: Say exactly what you do, in the customer's words

The top of your homepage has one job — confirm, in about two seconds, that the visitor is in the right place.

That means plain language. "Emergency plumbing and heating repairs in Bristol" beats "Delivering excellence in mechanical services since 2004." Nobody searches for excellence.

Use the words your customers use, not the words the trade uses. Homeowners don't search for "HVAC remediation," they search for "aircon not blowing cold." A roofer might call it a valley detail; the customer calls it a leak where two roofs meet. Write it their way.

A quick test: cover up your logo and read the first line of your homepage out loud. Could that line belong to a competitor in a different city, or even a different trade? If yes, it's too vague.

### Break your services into their own pages

One page listing twelve services is worse than twelve pages, one per service. Two reasons.

First, Google. Someone searching "drain unblocking" gets matched to a page about drain unblocking far more reliably than to a general "Our Services" page with a bullet point on it.

Second, the customer. A page dedicated to drain unblocking can talk about camera surveys, root ingress, how long a typical visit takes, what you do about the mess. That's reassurance a bullet point can't provide.

You don't need twelve pages on day one. Start with the three or four services that make you the most money, and add the rest over time.

## Essential #2: Make your service area unmistakable

The single most common failure on trade websites: no clear indication of where the business works.

If a customer can't tell in five seconds whether you cover their town, a chunk of them will leave rather than call to check. And the ones who do call and find out you don't cover them have just wasted your time too.

Put your service area in three places:

- **The homepage headline** — "Serving Reading, Wokingham, and Bracknell."
- **The footer of every page**, as a list of towns or postcode areas.
- **A dedicated areas page** if you cover a wide patch, listing towns properly.

If you cover several distinct towns and get real work from each, individual town pages can be worth it — but only if you write something genuinely specific for each one. A page about Guildford that mentions the older housing stock, the common boiler types you find there, jobs you've actually done there — that's useful. Twenty pages of the same paragraph with the town name swapped is spam, and Google treats it that way.

Also: put your address and phone number in text on the site, not baked into an image. Google reads text. It also needs your name, address, and phone number to match your Google Business Profile exactly. Same abbreviation, same format, same phone number. Small thing, real effect on local rankings.

## Essential #3: Proof that you're not going to make things worse

Hiring a tradesperson is a risk. The customer is letting a stranger into their home to do something they can't judge the quality of. Your site's job is to lower that fear.

Four things do the heavy lifting:

**Reviews.** Pull your best Google reviews onto the site, with the reviewer's first name and town. Not a slider of anonymous five-star quotes — those read as fake because half of them are. Better still, link to your actual Google profile so people can see the full picture.

**Photos of your own work.** This is the big one, and it's the thing most trades skip because it feels like a hassle. It isn't. Your phone is in your pocket. Before and after shots of a re-pointed chimney, a rewired consumer unit, a re-tiled bathroom — that's more persuasive than any paragraph you could write. Stock photos of a smiling model in a hard hat do the opposite; people recognise them instantly and it makes everything else look staged. When you're ready for a consistent set across your website, Google profile, social, and print, a professional [business photography](/blog/business-photography-near-me-houston/) and [video](/blog/business-video-production-near-me-houston/) shoot pays for itself fast.

**Credentials, honestly stated.** Gas Safe, NICEIC, Checkatrade, City & Guilds, public liability cover, DBS checks if you're working around families. Show the badges and put the registration number next to them. A number says you'll be checked. A logo alone says nothing.

**A face and a name.** "Run by Dave Marsh, 18 years in the trade, lives in Fareham." One paragraph and one honest photo on an About page. People hire people. A faceless company reads as a call centre that subcontracts to whoever's cheapest.

## Essential #4: A phone number that's genuinely easy to call

Most service enquiries still come by phone, and most of those come from a mobile.

Which means:

- Your number is in the header of every page, in a big readable size.
- It's a tap-to-call link, not plain text a customer has to memorise and retype.
- It's visible without scrolling, on a phone, on every page.
- On mobile, consider a sticky call button pinned to the bottom of the screen.

Also say when you answer. "Call 7am–7pm, seven days" or "Emergency call-outs 24/7" sets expectations. If you can't answer in the evening, say so — an unanswered phone loses more goodwill than a stated cut-off.

And if you're an emergency trade, be honest about response time. "Usually on site within two hours in the city" wins jobs. Don't promise it if you can't do it.

## Essential #5: A form that reaches you, and asks the right things

Some people won't call. They're at work, or they'd rather not talk, or it's 11pm. Give them a form.

Two rules.

**Keep it short.** Name, phone, postcode, and a box for "what's the problem." That's it. Every extra field loses people. You don't need their address, title, or how they heard about you — get that on the phone.

**Test that it works.** This sounds obvious. It is also the most common broken thing on small business websites. Forms silently break when a domain changes, or a host updates something, or a plugin expires. Submit your own form once a month from your phone. Check spam. I've seen businesses go months on a dead form, wondering why the website "doesn't work."

A WhatsApp or SMS link is worth adding too, especially for trades where a customer can just send a photo of the problem. A picture of a leaking joint saves you a diagnostic visit.

### What about online booking?

Depends on your trade.

If your work comes in standard slots — salons, dog grooming, mobile valeting, MOTs, personal training — online booking is a genuine upgrade. People book at night when you're closed, and it kills the phone tag.

If every job needs scoping — building work, landscaping, electrical rewires — booking is the wrong tool. You want an enquiry form and a callback. Trying to force a calendar onto quoted work creates awkward conversations and wasted trips.

## Essential #6: It has to be fast and work on a phone

Roughly speaking, most of your visitors will be on a phone, often on patchy data, often standing in the room with the problem.

What that means practically:

- **Speed.** Uncompressed photos straight off a camera are the usual culprit. A single oversized image can double your load time. If your site takes more than a few seconds, you're losing people before they see anything.
- **Readable text without pinching and zooming.** If you have to pinch, the site was designed on a desktop and never checked.
- **Buttons a thumb can hit.** Not tiny links crammed together.
- **HTTPS.** The padlock. Free to set up, and browsers now warn people off sites without it.

Do this: open your site on your own phone, outside, on mobile data, not wifi. Try to find your own phone number and submit your own form. That five-minute test tells you more than any report.

## The pages you actually need

A solid service business site is usually five to ten pages:

1. **Home** — what you do, where, proof, and how to reach you.
2. **A page per main service** — the money pages.
3. **About** — who you are, how long you've been at it, your face.
4. **Reviews / recent work** — photos and real reviews.
5. **Contact** — phone, form, hours, service area, address if you have premises.
6. **Areas covered** — one page, or town pages if you've got the material to write them properly.

That's it. That's a complete site for most trades.

## What you can skip

Being honest about what *doesn't* matter saves you money and time.

**A blog you won't maintain.** Three posts from two years ago look worse than no blog. Only start one if you'll genuinely keep it going, or someone's doing it for you.

**A team page with twelve stock headshots.** If you're two lads in a van, say you're two lads in a van. That's a selling point — the same person turns up every time.

**Animations and video backgrounds.** They slow the site and distract from the phone number.

**A chatbot.** For a local trade it usually annoys people who just wanted to call you.

**A big "Our Values" page.** Nobody chose a plumber based on their values statement. Show your work instead.

## The stuff outside your website that makes the website work

You can have a great site and still get no calls if these are missing.

**Google Business Profile.** For local service work this often drives more enquiries than the website itself. Claim it, fill in every field, pick the right categories, add photos regularly, answer every review. Your website supports the profile; the profile brings the volume. If you only have time for one thing this month, do this.

**Consistent details everywhere.** Same business name, address, and phone on your website, Google, Facebook, Checkatrade, Yell, and anywhere else you're listed. Inconsistencies confuse Google and customers both.

**Some way to know what's working.** Basic analytics, and ideally a way to see how many form submissions and calls come in. You don't need a dashboard. You need to know whether the site produced any work this month.

**Owning your own domain.** Not your web guy's account, not your nephew's. Yours, in your name, with the login in your email. Domains held by someone who's stopped answering the phone are one of the more painful messes to untangle.

## What drives the cost up or down

No numbers here, because every job's different — but here's what actually moves the price.

**Pushes cost up:**
- Lots of service pages and town pages, each needing original writing.
- Bespoke design instead of a proven layout.
- Booking systems, payment handling, quote calculators, customer portals.
- Copy written from scratch because you can't supply notes or details.
- Photography, if you don't have usable pictures of your own work.

**Keeps cost down:**
- A tight page count, expanded later as needed.
- Supplying your own job photos — genuinely the highest-value thing you can hand over.
- Ten minutes of voice notes on the phone about each service, instead of asking a writer to invent it.
- Starting with the essentials and adding once you see what customers actually use.

**Watch out for:** what happens after launch. Ask who owns the domain, who has the logins, whether hosting and updates are included, what a small text change costs, and whether you can take the site elsewhere. A cheap build with no exit is expensive later.

## A sensible order to do this in

If you're starting from nothing or rebuilding, this order gets you results fastest:

1. Claim and fill in your Google Business Profile. Free, and it moves the needle immediately.
2. **Get a one-page site up:** what you do, where, phone number, form, a handful of job photos. Live beats perfect. If photos are thin, schedule a shoot so the site and Google profile launch strong.
3. **Add a page for each of your top services.**
4. **Add reviews and a proper gallery of your own work** — and matching print (cards, yard signs) once the digital brand is locked.
5. Add service area pages if they're worth writing properly.
6. Then, if it fits your trade, look at booking or quoting tools.

Most businesses get 80% of the benefit from steps one through three. Don't let step six hold up step one.

## The honest summary

A good website for a local service business isn't clever. It says what you do, where you do it, shows that you've done it before, and makes it stupidly easy to get hold of you. It loads fast on a phone and the form works.

That's a low bar, and most competitors don't clear it. Which is good news for you.
