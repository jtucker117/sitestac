## The short answer

Every small business website needs five pages to function: a **home page**, a **services page** (with a separate page for each major service), an **about page**, a **contact page**, and a **reviews page**. After that, what you add depends on your trade — a roofer needs city pages, a restaurant needs a menu, a salon needs online booking.

Most small sites land somewhere between 8 and 20 pages. If somebody tells you that you need 60, they're either selling you something or they've never run a service business.

Let's walk through the whole thing.

---

## The five pages every business needs

These aren't optional. Skip one and you'll feel it — either in your phone not ringing or in people calling with questions the site should have answered.

### 1. The home page

Your home page has about five seconds to tell a stranger three things: what you do, where you do it, and how to reach you.

That's it. Not your company values. Not a stock photo of a handshake. A plumber's home page should say something like "Licensed plumbers in Bakersfield — drain cleaning, water heaters, repipes. Same-day service." Phone number big, at the top, clickable on a phone.

The rest of the home page is a summary of everything else on your site: a short list of services with links, a few reviews, a photo or two of actual work, a service area, and a form. Think of it like the front counter of a shop. You're pointing people where they need to go.

One thing owners get wrong constantly: writing the home page about themselves instead of the customer. "Founded in 1998, we are a family-owned company committed to excellence" tells a guy with water on his kitchen floor nothing. "Burst pipe? We're there today" tells him everything.

### 2. A services page — and a page per service

This is where most small business sites fall apart, so pay attention here.

You need one page that lists everything you do, and then **a separate page for each service you actually want to sell**. Not a bullet list. A real page.

Here's why. When somebody searches "water heater replacement Bakersfield," Google wants to send them to a page about water heater replacement in Bakersfield. If you've got one services page that mentions water heaters in a bullet alongside twelve other things, you're competing against plumbers who built a whole page on it. You lose.

An HVAC company might have:

- AC repair
- AC installation
- Furnace repair
- Furnace installation
- Ductwork
- Maintenance plans
- Indoor air quality

That's seven service pages plus the overview. Each one covers what the job involves, what it usually costs *in relative terms* (what makes it more or less), how long it takes, what brands you work with, and what happens when you show up.

Same logic for everyone else. An electrician: panel upgrades, EV charger installs, rewiring, generators, lighting. A landscaper: lawn maintenance, irrigation, hardscaping, tree work, cleanups. A salon: cuts, color, extensions, treatments.

**Rule of thumb:** if somebody could search for it by name and you'd want that call, it gets its own page.

Don't build pages for services you don't want. A roofer who hates doing gutter cleaning shouldn't have a gutter cleaning page attracting gutter cleaning calls.

### 3. The about page

The about page is more important for trades than people think. You're asking a stranger to let you into their house. They want to see a face.

Good about pages have:

- A real photo of you, your crew, your truck — not stock
- How long you've been doing this and how you got into it
- Licenses, bonding, insurance, certifications
- Who's actually going to show up at the door

A two-sentence paragraph about "our commitment to quality" is worthless. A paragraph that says "I started hanging drywall with my dad at 16, got my contractor's license in 2009, and I still run every job myself" is worth a lot.

If you've got a team, put their names and faces up. Customers who've had a bad contractor experience are scanning for signs you're a real operation.

### 4. The contact page

This page has one job: make it stupid easy to reach you.

What belongs on it:

- Phone number, clickable
- A short form — name, phone, what they need. That's enough. Every extra field drops responses.
- Email address if you check it
- Hours, including whether you take emergency calls
- Service area, stated plainly
- Your address and a map **if** you have a location people visit

That last one matters. A restaurant, salon, or shop needs an address and a map. A mobile plumber working out of a truck doesn't need to publish his home address — just the service area.

Also: your phone number should be in the header of every page, not just here. People shouldn't have to hunt.

### 5. Reviews and testimonials

People check reviews before they call. If they're already on your site, give them what they're looking for instead of sending them back to Google where your competitor's ad is sitting.

Pull your best reviews from Google, Yelp, Facebook, Angi — wherever they live — and put them on a page. Use first names and the city. "Dana R., Oakdale — they rerouted the gas line in one afternoon and cleaned up after themselves" reads real. "Great service! — A customer" reads fake.

If you're newer and don't have many, start asking. Every finished job, text the customer a direct link. It takes 30 seconds and it compounds.

## Pages that earn their keep, depending on your business

Now the ones that depend on what you do.

### Location pages (if you serve multiple towns)

If you cover a metro area, you want a page for each city or neighborhood you actually want work in. A garage door company in the Twin Cities might have pages for Minneapolis, St. Paul, Bloomington, Edina, Maple Grove.

These only work if they're real pages. Copying one page and swapping the city name is the oldest trick in the book and it stopped working years ago. A good location page mentions neighborhoods, references actual jobs you've done there, notes anything specific about housing stock in that area — older homes with galvanized pipe, newer builds with a certain kind of panel.

If you can't write something genuinely specific about a town, don't make a page for it. Three good ones beat twenty thin ones.

### A gallery or portfolio

If your work is visual, show it. Roofers, landscapers, remodelers, painters, tile setters, salons, tattoo shops — your photos sell the job better than any paragraph.

Before-and-after is the strongest format. Take the photos with your phone, consistent angle, decent light. You don't need a photographer. You need a habit.

For some trades — drain cleaning, HVAC repair — a gallery does less. Nobody's choosing a plumber based on a photo of a clean trap. Skip it or keep it small.

### A menu page (restaurants)

Non-negotiable for food. And it has to be **text on a page**, not a PDF and not a photo of a printed menu.

This trips up a lot of restaurant owners. A PDF menu is hard to read on a phone, it doesn't show up properly in search, and it's a pain to update. Real text on a real page means someone searching "birria tacos near me" can actually find you.

Update it when prices change. Nothing annoys a customer faster than a menu that doesn't match the bill.

### A booking page (salons, spas, studios)

If you take appointments, let people book without calling. Younger customers in particular will leave before they'll dial a phone.

This usually means embedding whatever system you already use — Square, Vagaro, Booksy, Calendly. One page, one button, done. If you've got multiple stylists or service lengths, the booking tool handles it. Don't rebuild it on your site.

### Pricing or financing

Touchy subject. Most trades can't publish flat prices because every job is different, and a number on a public page becomes a promise someone will hold you to.

What you *can* do is explain how pricing works. Do you charge a diagnostic fee? Is it flat-rate or time and materials? What drives a job up — access, permits, old materials, emergency hours? Do you offer financing, and who's the lender?

This page answers the question everybody has and nobody wants to ask first. It also filters out tire kickers before they eat an hour of your day.

### An FAQ page

Write down the ten questions you answer on the phone every single week and put them on a page.

"Do you charge for estimates?" "Are you licensed and insured?" "How fast can you get here?" "Do you work weekends?" "Do I need a permit?"

Two benefits: fewer repeat phone calls, and these are the exact phrasings people type into Google. It's one of the highest-return pages you can build and it takes an afternoon.

### A blog or advice section

Worth it if you'll actually keep it going. Dead weight if you won't.

The posts that work for trades are the practical ones: "How to tell if your water heater is about to fail," "What a roof inspection actually covers," "Why your AC freezes up in August." You're answering the questions people search *before* they're ready to hire. Some of them hire you later.

One genuinely useful post a month beats twelve rushed ones. If you can't commit to that, put your energy into service pages instead — they convert better anyway.

### A careers page

If you're hiring — and most trades are — a careers page is cheap and it works. Describe the roles, pay structure in general terms, what the day looks like, and what you expect. Good techs check your website before they apply.

### An emergency or 24-hour page

If you take after-hours calls, give it a page. "Emergency plumber in Tucson" is its own search with its own urgency, and the person doing that search at 11pm is going to call whoever shows up first. Make it obvious you answer the phone.

## The boring but necessary pages

Nobody reads these. You still need them.

**Privacy policy.** If you have a contact form, you collect personal information. A privacy policy explains what you do with it. Required by various laws, expected by ad platforms, and it takes five minutes.

**Terms of service.** Less critical for a service business than an online store, but useful if you sell anything online or run promotions.

**Accessibility statement.** Shows you've made an effort and gives people a way to flag problems. Worth having.

**A 404 page.** When someone hits a dead link, don't dump them on a blank error screen. Point them back to your services and your phone number.

**A thank-you page.** After someone submits a form, send them somewhere that confirms it went through and tells them when you'll call back. It also lets you track how many leads you're getting, which you'll want later.

## How to decide what you actually need

Forget page counts. Ask one question:

**What would someone type into Google to find this, and is there a page that answers it?**

If a customer searches "AC not blowing cold Phoenix," is there a page on your site about AC repair in Phoenix? If somebody searches "are you licensed," does your site answer that? If they search your business name and "reviews," do they find something?

Every page should map to a real question or a real job. If you can't name the search that leads to a page, you probably don't need it.

The flip side is also true. Pages that exist for no reason — "Our Philosophy," "Mission Statement," "Welcome" — dilute the site and give visitors places to wander off. Cut them.

## Common mistakes

**Cramming everything onto one page.** One-page sites look clean and rank terribly. You've got one page competing for fifteen different searches and winning none.

**One page for all services.** Covered above. This is the single most common problem on small business sites.

**Burying the phone number.** It should be in the header on every page, clickable on mobile. Not in the footer. Not on the contact page only.

**Contact forms with twelve fields.** Name, phone, what they need. Add fields and watch submissions drop.

**PDFs instead of pages.** Menus, price lists, service brochures — put them on a page as text.

**Ignoring mobile.** Most of your traffic is on a phone, often standing in a flooded basement or sitting in a parking lot. If your site is hard to use with one thumb, none of the rest matters.

**Building thin city pages.** Twenty copy-pasted location pages with the town name swapped will do nothing. Build the three or four you genuinely care about and make them good.

## What order to build in

If you're starting from zero, here's the sequence:

1. **Home, contact, and your top three services.** This is a working site. It can take calls. Launch it.
2. **About and reviews.** Trust layer. Add within the first few weeks.
3. **The rest of your service pages.** One at a time, best-selling first.
4. **Location pages** for the towns you want more work in.
5. **FAQ, gallery, financing** — whatever matches your trade.
6. **Blog**, if and only if you'll keep it up.

Don't wait until everything's perfect to go live. A five-page site that's live today beats a twenty-page site that launches in four months.

---

## One more thing

Pages aren't a one-time build. Add a new service? New page. Expand into a neighboring town? New page. Keep getting the same question on the phone? Add it to the FAQ.

The sites that do well are the ones that grow a little every few months. The ones that stall are the ones built once and never touched again.
