## The short answer

Pick the shortest, easiest-to-say version of your business name that's still available, add your trade or city only if you need to, and end it in .com if you can get it. If someone hears your domain on the radio or over the phone, they should be able to type it correctly on the first try. That's the whole test.

Everything below is just how to get there without painting yourself into a corner.

## Start with your actual business name

If your truck says "Keller Plumbing," your domain should be kellerplumbing.com. Not keller-plumbing-services-llc.com. Not kellerplumbingco2024.com.

People already know your name. They're going to type it into the address bar or into Google. Make the thing they type land on your site.

If the exact match is taken — and for common last names it often is — you have a few good moves before you start inventing weird spellings:

- **Add your city or region.** kellerplumbingdenver.com, or denverkellerplumbing.com. This also helps a little with local search, though not as much as people claim.
- **Add the trade if it's not already in the name.** If you're "Sandoval & Sons," sandovalroofing.com tells people what you do.
- **Add a natural word.** "the," "go," "hire," "call." callkellerplumbing.com reads fine on a truck wrap.

What you want to avoid is anything that requires explanation. If you ever find yourself saying "it's Keller Plumbing but with a 'z' instead of an 's'" — start over.

## The out-loud test

This is the one rule that catches most bad domain names. Say it out loud to someone who's never heard it, and have them write it down.

Things that fail this test constantly:

**Hyphens.** keller-plumbing.com sounds identical to kellerplumbing.com when you say it. Nobody hears the dash. You'll lose traffic to whoever owns the non-hyphen version.

**Numbers.** Is it "4" or "four"? Is it "24/7" or "247"? A1 Garage vs. A-One Garage. Pick a lane and you still lose half the people.

**Words that get spelled two ways.** Pro vs. Pros. Co vs. Company. Center vs. Centre. Lite vs. Light.

**Letter pileups.** When two words smash together and create a third word, you have a problem. Classic examples: a pen seller at penisland.com, or a therapist at therapistfinder.com. Say your domain slowly as one string before you buy it.

## Length matters more than keywords

There's an old idea floating around that stuffing keywords into your domain helps you rank — that bestdenverplumbingrepair.com will beat kellerplumbing.com on Google. That hasn't been true for over a decade. Google figured out that trick and shut it down.

What a long keyword domain actually does:

- Gets typed wrong
- Looks spammy on a business card
- Reads terribly in an email address (jim@bestdenverplumbingrepair.com)
- Makes you sound like a lead-gen site instead of a real local company

Shorter is almost always better. Two words beats four. If you can get it under about 20 characters, you're in good shape.

## Pick your ending (.com, .net, .co, and the rest)

Get the .com if you can. It's what people default to when they're guessing, and it's what they assume you have. If someone's trying to find you and types your name plus .com and hits someone else's site, that's a lead you'll never know you lost.

If the .com is genuinely gone and the owner won't sell at a sane number, your next-best options are the ones people won't second-guess: .co, or a location-specific one like .ca or .co.uk if that's where you operate. Trade-specific endings like .plumbing or .construction exist, but most customers have never seen one and will assume they typed it wrong.

Skip .net, .biz, and .info. They read as either dated or sketchy to most people.

One more thing: if you buy kellerplumbingdenver.com, it's worth grabbing the obvious close variations too, and pointing them at your main site. Cheap insurance against typos and against a competitor picking up your near-twin.

## Check these before you buy

Take ten minutes and run these checks. It's much easier than renaming yourself in two years.

**Is the business name itself taken?** Search your state's business registry and do a basic trademark search. A domain doesn't give you rights to a name, and buying one that infringes on an existing trademark is how you end up with a letter from a lawyer.

**Does the name have history?** Paste the domain into archive.org's Wayback Machine. If it used to be a spam site or an adult site, that's baggage you don't want. Rare, but worth the thirty seconds.

**Are the social handles open?** You probably want the matching Facebook, Instagram, and Google Business Profile names. Consistency makes you look like a real operation.

**Does the email address read right?** You'll be putting it on invoices and estimates for years. Type it out and look at it.

## Own it yourself

This is the part that bites people. Whoever registers the domain controls it. If your nephew or a previous web guy registered it under their own account and then disappears, you can be stuck — and getting a domain back from someone who won't answer the phone is a miserable process.

Register it in your own name, with your own email and your own credit card, at a registrar you can log into. If someone else is setting it up for you, make sure the account is in your name and you have the login. Any honest web company will hand that over without a fight.

Also: turn on auto-renew, and keep the card on file current. Expired domains get snapped up fast, sometimes within hours.

## You can't undo this easily

Changing a domain later isn't impossible, but it's a real project. You have to redirect the old one, update every listing and directory you're in, reprint cards and truck lettering, and ride out a dip in search traffic while Google sorts out the move.

So spend an afternoon on it now. Say your top three choices out loud, text them to a few people, and see which one comes back typed correctly.
