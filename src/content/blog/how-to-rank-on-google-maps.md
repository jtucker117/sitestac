## The short answer

To rank on Google Maps, you need three things: a fully filled-out Google Business Profile with the right primary category, a steady flow of recent reviews, and a website that backs up what your profile claims you do. Google ranks map results mostly on relevance, distance, and prominence — you can't move your shop, so you win on the other two.

Everything else in this post is detail on how to do that without wasting six months.

## How Google actually picks the top three

When someone searches "plumber near me," Google shows a map with three businesses above the regular results. That's the local pack, and it's where most of the calls come from. Almost nobody scrolls past it.

Google decides who lands there using three factors it's openly stated:

**Relevance.** How well does your profile match what was typed? If someone searches "emergency plumber" and your profile just says "Plumber" with no mention of emergency or 24-hour service anywhere, you're less relevant than the guy who spelled it out.

**Distance.** How close are you to the searcher, or to the area they named? You can't fake this. A shop in the north end will struggle to rank for searches happening downtown. This is why chasing the whole metro area is a losing game and owning your own few neighborhoods is not.

**Prominence.** How well known and well regarded are you? Reviews, mentions on other sites, links to your website, how long you've been listed. This is the one you build over time.

You control relevance and prominence. Start there.

## Step one: fix the profile before anything else

Most businesses I look at are losing on basics. If you haven't claimed and verified your listing yet, start with our [step-by-step guide to getting your business on Google](/blog/how-to-get-your-business-to-show-up-on-google-local-seo-basics/) and come back here.

Once it's claimed, go through it line by line:

**Primary category is the single biggest lever.** Google weights it heavily. "Electrician" and "Electrical installation service" pull different searches. Pick the one that matches what you want to be called for, then add secondary categories for the rest of what you do. Don't stuff twelve categories in — three or four relevant ones beat a pile of loose ones.

**Services and descriptions.** List every service individually. A roofer shouldn't just say "Roofing." List shingle replacement, flat roof repair, gutter installation, storm damage inspection. Each one is a phrase somebody types.

**Hours, including holidays.** Google demotes profiles it thinks might be closed or abandoned. Keep them accurate.

**Photos.** Real ones, from your phone, of actual jobs. Ten photos of finished work beats one stock image of a smiling stranger holding a wrench. Add new ones monthly — it's a signal the business is active.

**Service area.** If you go to customers instead of them coming to you, set your service area honestly. Listing every town in the county doesn't make you rank in every town; it just dilutes you.

## Step two: reviews, and the part everyone skips

Reviews are the closest thing to a ranking cheat code, but the details matter.

**Recency counts more than total.** A shop with 40 reviews from this year outranks one with 200 reviews that stopped three years ago. You want a trickle, not a flood.

**Ask every single customer, at the right moment.** Right after the job, while they're happy and standing next to your work. Not by email three weeks later. Text them a direct link to your review page from your phone before you pull out of the driveway.

**Reply to all of them.** Good and bad. Google notices, and so do the people reading. A calm reply to a one-star review does more for you than the review costs you.

**Never buy them.** Google is good at spotting purchased reviews now, and the penalty is your whole profile getting suspended. Not worth it.

One more thing: when customers mention the service and the town in their review — "They fixed our water heater in Bellingham same day" — that text helps you rank for those terms. You can't script it, but you can ask the right question. "Would you mind mentioning what we did and where?" is enough.

## Step three: your website has to hold up its end

This is where a lot of businesses stall. They polish the Google profile, get some reviews, and then plateau — because there's nothing behind it.

Google cross-references your profile with your website. If your profile says you do commercial HVAC in three cities and your website is a single page that says "Heating and Cooling — Call Us," there's nothing for Google to confirm.

What helps:

- **A page per major service.** Not a bullet list — an actual page explaining the work, what it involves, what you charge for in general terms.
- **A page per town you seriously serve.** Real content about that area, not the same paragraph with the city name swapped. Google can spot the swap.
- **Your name, address, and phone number in the same format everywhere.** On your site, your profile, your Facebook page, your listing on the local chamber site. "Suite 4" on one and "#4" on another is a small mismatch, but they add up.
- **A site that loads fast on a phone.** Most local searches happen on a phone, often standing in a flooded basement. A slow site loses the call and, over time, loses ranking.

## What doesn't work

Saving you some time and money:

**Fake locations.** Renting a mailbox in the next town to rank there gets profiles suspended, and getting reinstated is a slog.

**Keyword-stuffing your business name.** "Dave's Plumbing — Best Emergency Plumber Seattle Drain Cleaning" violates the guidelines and competitors will report you. Use your real name.

**Cheap directory blasts.** Services that promise to list you on 500 directories mostly create junk listings with slightly wrong details, which works against you.

**Posting daily to Google Posts.** They're worth using occasionally, but they aren't a ranking factor worth building a routine around.

## How long this takes

Profile fixes can shift things in a few weeks. Reviews and website work compound over months. If you're in a competitive trade in a big city, expect to be at it for a while before you crack the top three. In a smaller town with three competitors, you might get there in a season just by doing the basics properly.

The advantage is that most of your competitors won't keep it up. The ones who ask for a review on every job and add a service page every couple months pull ahead and stay there.
