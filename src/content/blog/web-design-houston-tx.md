## The short answer

Good web design in Houston, TX means a fast, mobile-first site that makes it obvious what you do, where you'll drive to, and how to reach you — backed by separate pages for the suburbs and neighborhoods you actually serve. Houston is too spread out for one generic "Houston" page to win work in Katy, Kingwood, and Pearland at the same time. The businesses that show up in local search here are the ones that treat their service area like a map, not a single dot.

Below is what that looks like in practice, and what it costs you when you skip it.

![Houston is a driving market: inside loop, Beltway 8, suburbs](/blog/web-design-houston-tx/hero.webp)

## Houston is a driving market, and your website should say so

This metro sprawls. A homeowner in Cypress and a homeowner in Clear Lake are both "in Houston" and they're nowhere near each other. When either one pulls out a phone and types "AC repair near me," Google leans hard on where they're standing — not on the word Houston.

So if your site is one page that says "Serving Houston and surrounding areas," you're invisible to most of the people searching for you. What works better is a real page for each area you'll actually drive to: Katy, Sugar Land, Pearland, The Woodlands, Spring, Cypress, Missouri City, Baytown, Pasadena, Humble, League City, whatever your list is.

And those pages have to say something. A page that's the same paragraph with the city name swapped is worse than no page at all — Google's seen that trick for years. Write about the jobs you've done there. Slab foundations in older neighborhoods versus newer builds out past the Grand Parkway. Drive time and whether that area gets your emergency rate. Photos from a job on that side of town.

Be honest about your radius, too. If you won't cross Beltway 8 for a service call, don't build a page for Conroe. You'll just book calls you have to turn down.

## Google Business Profile does more work than your homepage

For most local trades and service businesses here, the map pack is where the phone calls come from. Your Google Business Profile is the thing ranking there, and your website supports it.

A few things that matter more in a metro this size:

- **Your listed address anchors your radius.** A shop in Spring Branch will have a harder time ranking for Friendswood searches than a shop in Pearland will, no matter how good the website is. That's not something a web designer can fix with words — it's geography. Build your service area pages to cover the gap.
- **Reviews that mention neighborhoods help.** When customers write "replaced our water heater in the Heights," that's a signal. You can't script reviews, but you can ask at the right moment.
- **Your name, address, and phone need to match everywhere.** Website footer, Google, Facebook, Yelp, the chamber listing you forgot about in 2019.

The website's job is to back all that up: same phone number, clear service list, real photos, and pages that prove you work where you say you work.

## Storm season changes what your site has to do

From June through November, and honestly any time a line of storms rolls through, demand for certain trades spikes overnight. Roofers, tree services, restoration and water mitigation crews, fence companies, generator installers, electricians — the phone goes from normal to unmanageable in a few hours.

A website built for that looks different. It has:

- A banner or notice you can flip on yourself, without calling anyone, that says whether you're taking new jobs and what the current wait looks like
- A short intake form that captures address, damage type, and whether insurance is involved — so your office isn't spending fifteen minutes on the phone to learn it's out of your area
- Hosting that doesn't fall over when traffic jumps

Same goes for the freeze weeks. Plumbers in this market know exactly what a hard freeze does to their call volume. If your site can't communicate "we're at capacity, here's the form, we'll call in order," you lose the jobs you *could* have taken because you were drowning in calls you couldn't.

Houston's other constant is water — drainage, French drains, sump work, flood-related repair. If you do that work, say so plainly. People search for it directly.

## Speed and phone-first, because nobody's reading this on a desktop

Most of your traffic is a phone. Half of it is somebody sitting in a truck in a parking lot with two bars, or standing in a hot garage looking at a dead compressor. If your site takes six seconds to load, they hit back and call the next guy.

That means: small image files, no heavy sliders, no video autoplaying on the homepage. Phone number tappable in the top corner on every page. Service list readable without pinching. Contact form short enough to fill out with a thumb.

This is the single most common thing we fix on Houston sites that aren't producing. The design isn't bad — it's just heavy.

## Language matters here more than most places

Houston is one of the most diverse metros in the country, and a meaningful share of your customers are more comfortable reading in Spanish, Vietnamese, or another language. If a good chunk of your existing customer base already speaks Spanish with your crew, having key pages available in Spanish isn't a nice-to-have — it's the difference between a call and a bounce.

You don't need the whole site translated. Homepage, main services, and contact usually covers it. Just make sure it's real translation, not a browser plugin that mangles your service names.

## Show local work, not stock photos

Stock photography of a smiling technician in a spotless uniform reads as fake to a Houston homeowner who's had three contractors ghost them. Your crew's actual photos — a re-piped attic, a new roof on a brick ranch, a repaired slab, a restaurant patio you built — do more selling than any headline.

Put the city or neighborhood in the caption. That's free local relevance and it's honest. If your existing shots aren't strong enough, schedule a [professional photo and video shoot](/blog/business-photography-near-me-houston/) and reuse those assets on the site, Google profile, social, and [print](/blog/custom-print-design-business-cards-yard-signs/).

## What drives the cost up or down

Nobody can quote a Houston website without knowing the job, same as you can't quote a re-roof over the phone. But here's what moves the number:

**Pushes it up:** a long list of service area pages, multiple languages, online booking or scheduling that talks to your dispatch software, e-commerce, heavy photo and video work, complex quote calculators.

**Keeps it down:** a tight service list, using photos you already have, sticking to the pages that actually get searched instead of building twenty you'll never update.

**Watch for:** hosting and maintenance billed separately after the fact, and who owns the domain and the site files when the relationship ends. Ask that up front. If the answer isn't "you do," keep looking.

## The mistakes we see most

One page trying to cover the whole metro. A phone number buried in the footer. Service area lists with thirty cities and no page behind any of them. Sites nobody's touched since the last owner ran the marketing. And the big one — a beautiful site that never got connected to a Google Business Profile, so it sits there looking good and ringing nothing.
