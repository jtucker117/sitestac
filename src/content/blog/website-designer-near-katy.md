Searching "website designer near Katy" makes sense, but proximity isn't really what you're shopping for. What matters is whether the person building your site understands how Katy customers search, how the area's county lines mess with your Google listing, and how to beat the Houston-wide companies that run ads into your zip code every day. A designer two miles away who doesn't know that is worth less than one across town who does.

Here's how to tell the difference.

## "Near Katy" is a bigger area than most people think

The City of Katy itself is small. The thing everybody *calls* Katy — the sprawl along I-10 and the Grand Parkway, the master-planned neighborhoods, the strip centers on Mason and Fry — is mostly unincorporated land spread across Harris, Fort Bend, and Waller counties.

This matters more than it sounds. Plenty of businesses with a Katy mailing address end up showing a Houston or Fulshear context in Google's eyes. Customers type "Katy" into their phone. Your listing, your service area settings, and your site content all have to line up with that word even when your official address doesn't say it.

A designer who works this market already knows to ask about it. One who doesn't will build you a nice-looking site that quietly fails to show up when a homeowner off Cinco Ranch Boulevard searches for what you do.

Ask any candidate how they'd handle it. If the answer is vague, keep looking.

## You're not just competing with other Katy shops

This is the part that catches owners off guard. When someone in Katy searches for an AC repair, a roofer, or a foundation guy, the results aren't limited to businesses based in Katy. Every large Houston operator with a service-area listing and a budget is bidding for that same screen.

So the fight isn't "my site vs. the shop on the other side of the Grand Parkway." It's "my site vs. a company with a call center and a marketing department."

The good news: those big operators are usually generic. Their pages say "Greater Houston" and list forty cities in the footer. Yours can say Katy, name the neighborhoods and subdivisions you actually work in, show photos of jobs with recognizable Texas rooflines and clay-soil driveways, and name the response time you can really hit from your shop.

Specific beats big more often than you'd expect. But only if your designer builds for it on purpose.

## Katy customers search from a phone, usually under pressure

The work that pays the bills around here is heavy on home services — HVAC, plumbing, roofing, foundation repair, pool service, irrigation, tree work, garage doors, pest control, remodelers. Add the restaurants, salons, med spas, and gyms filling the retail along the major corridors.

For the trades, most of the searches that turn into money happen during a problem. AC quits in August. Hot water heater lets go. Storm season rolls through and a shingle's missing. That person is standing in their garage holding a phone, and they're calling two or three numbers until someone picks up.

That changes what a good site looks like. It needs to load fast on cell service, show a tappable phone number without scrolling, and answer two questions immediately: do you cover my area, and can you come today. Everything else — the About page, the history, the awards — is secondary. We dug into the same logic for the trades in [this piece on plumber website design](/blog/plumber-website-design-sugar-land/), and it holds anywhere in west Houston.

For restaurants and salons the pressure is different but the speed isn't. Hours, menu or service list, and a working booking or ordering link. People decide on dinner in about ninety seconds.

## What being local actually buys you — and what it doesn't

Let's be honest about this. A website can be built from anywhere. The code doesn't know where it was written.

What a nearby designer genuinely gives you:

- **They can show up.** Photos of your trucks, your shop, your crew, your finished work. Real photos outperform stock every time, and most owners never get around to taking them without someone pushing.
- **They understand the geography.** They know Katy addresses cross county lines, that Fulshear and Brookshire and Cypress all bleed into your service area, and which of those are worth a page and which aren't.
- **They've seen the seasons.** Storm season, the August AC crush, the holiday rush for restaurants. They know when your site needs to be ready.
- **You can get them on the phone.** Not a ticket system in another time zone.

What it doesn't buy you: anything magic for rankings. Google doesn't care where your developer sits. It cares about your business location, your reviews, and whether your site actually answers what people searched for. If the best fit for you is based in Sugar Land or Houston and clearly knows this market, that's fine. ## How to vet someone in about twenty minutes

Skip the portfolio screenshots. Do this instead.

**Pull up three sites they built, on your phone, on cell data.** Not wifi. Count the seconds. If you're waiting, your customers are leaving.

**Try to call from those sites.** Is the number tappable? How many taps from landing to dialing? Should be one or two.

**Search for those businesses by name.** Do they come up clean — listing, reviews, site? If the designer's own clients are hard to find, that tells you something.

**Ask who owns the site.** Domain, hosting, content, images. The answer should be you, unambiguously, with no drama if you leave. This is where people get burned.

**Ask what happens after launch.** Who changes your hours on a holiday? Who adds the new service you started offering? If the answer is "submit a request," ask how long that takes.

**Ask what they'd do about your Google Business Profile.** A site with no listing behind it is a car with no wheels. If they treat that as somebody else's job, you'll be doing it yourself.

## What moves the price

Nobody can quote you honestly off a keyword. But the drivers are predictable.

A single-location shop with four or five services and a clear service area is the simple end. Cost climbs when you add real page depth — separate pages per service and per area instead of one list — plus online booking, e-commerce, menu systems that sync, multiple locations, or a photo shoot.

The quieter cost is what *isn't* included. Hosting, a domain, an SSL certificate, basic security updates, and small text edits should be part of the deal or clearly priced. Content is the one most people underestimate — if nobody's writing the words, either you are, or your site ends up with the same filler paragraphs as everyone else's. We broke the pieces down further in [this look at what local customers actually check](/blog/web-design-sugar-land-tx/).

Cheapest bid usually means a template and a copy-paste. You'll pay the difference later in lost calls.

Hire someone who asks about your service area before they ask about colors. Who knows a Katy address isn't always a Katy address as far as Google's concerned. Who builds for a guy in a hot garage with one bar of signal, not for a design award.
