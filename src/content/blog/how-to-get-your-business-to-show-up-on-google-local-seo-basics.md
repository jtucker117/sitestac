## The short answer

To get your business on Google, create a free Google Business Profile at google.com/business, verify that you're the real owner (usually by postcard, phone, or video), then fill the profile out completely — correct category, hours, service area, phone number, photos, and a link to your website. Verification takes anywhere from a few days to a couple of weeks, and once you're verified you'll start appearing in Google Maps and local search results.

That's the ten-minute version. But "on Google" and "found on Google" are two different things. Plenty of plumbers have a verified profile that nobody ever sees. The rest of this covers how to actually get in front of people who are searching for what you do.

## The three places you can show up

When someone searches "drain cleaning near me," Google shows them a page with three distinct sections. Understanding which is which saves you a lot of confusion later.

**Ads at the top.** Paid. You bid, you pay per click. Some of these are Local Services Ads with the green "Google Guaranteed" badge, which is a separate program for home service trades.

**The map pack.** The little map with three business listings under it. This is driven entirely by your Google Business Profile. For local service businesses, this is the most valuable real estate on the page — it's above the regular results and it's where the phone calls come from.

**Organic results.** The regular blue links below the map. These come from your website. If you don't have one, you can't be here.

Most small businesses should be chasing the map pack first, then organic results, then ads once the first two are working. Ads are the fastest way to appear and the fastest way to spend money on nothing if the rest of your setup is weak.

## Step 1: Create your Google Business Profile

Go to google.com/business and sign in with a Google account. Use one that belongs to the business, not your nephew's Gmail from when he set up your Facebook page in 2016. You will need access to this account forever.

Search for your business name first. If somebody already created a listing — and Google auto-generates listings all the time from public data — claim it instead of making a duplicate. Duplicates split your reviews and confuse Google about which one is real.

Then work through the setup:

**Business name.** Your actual business name. Not "Miller Plumbing — Emergency Plumber 24/7 Lowest Prices." Google's guidelines prohibit keyword stuffing in the name field, and competitors report it constantly. Getting suspended over this is a genuinely bad week.

**Category.** This one matters more than almost anything else you'll enter. Your primary category tells Google which searches you're eligible to appear in. A roofer who picks "Contractor" instead of "Roofing Contractor" will get buried. Pick the most specific primary category that describes your main service, then add secondary categories for the other things you do — but don't add twenty. If you're mainly an electrician who also does generator installs, primary is "Electrician," secondary is "Generator Supplier."

**Address or service area.** If customers come to you — a salon, a restaurant, a repair shop — enter your street address. If you go to them, hide the address and set a service area instead. You can list up to twenty areas, but keep it honest. A landscaper in one suburb listing every town within ninety minutes is diluting the signal, not expanding reach. Google generally shows you to people near your actual location anyway.

**Phone number.** Use a local number if you have one. A local area code is a small trust signal to both Google and the customer. If you use call tracking, make the tracked number the primary and put your real number as an additional number — otherwise you break the consistency Google looks for.

**Website.** Link it. If you don't have one, more on that below.

## Step 2: Get verified

Until you're verified, your profile doesn't really exist to customers. Google offers a few methods and doesn't always let you choose:

- **Postcard** to your business address with a code. Slowest option, usually a week or two.
- **Phone or text** with a code. Fast, but not offered to everyone.
- **Email.** Rare.
- **Video verification.** Increasingly common. Google asks you to record a continuous, unedited video showing your location, your equipment, your signage, and you accessing something that proves you run the place — a key to the shop, your branded van, your tools, your point-of-sale system.

For video verification, film it in one take, don't stop recording, and show the street sign or building number first so Google can match your location. Have your van with the logo, your gear, and something with your business name on it ready before you hit record. Most rejections happen because the video is edited or doesn't clearly connect the person filming to the business.

If verification gets rejected, you can appeal. Be patient and don't create a second listing while you wait — that's how you end up with duplicates and a suspension.

## Step 3: Fill it out completely — not partially

A verified but half-empty profile is the most common problem we see. Google rewards complete profiles, and so do customers. Every field you skip is a reason for someone to call the next guy.

**Hours.** Accurate, including holidays. If you take emergency calls at 2 a.m., set your hours to 24 hours and say so — don't leave it at 8–5 and hope people call anyway.

**Services.** There's a section where you can list individual services with short descriptions. Fill it out. A HVAC company should have separate entries for AC installation, furnace repair, duct cleaning, maintenance plans, and so on. This is free space to tell Google exactly what you do in the same words customers use.

**Products.** Useful for restaurants, salons, and retail. A salon can list cuts, color, extensions, and treatments with photos.

**Business description.** 750 characters. Write it like you'd explain your business to a new neighbor. Mention what you do, where you work, and what makes you different. Skip the corporate language.

**Attributes.** Things like "woman-owned," "wheelchair accessible," "free estimates," "online appointments." Small, but they show up as filters in search and some people use them.

**Opening date.** If you've been around since 1998, say so. Longevity converts.

## Step 4: Photos, and lots of them

Profiles with real photos get more clicks and more calls. Not stock photos — actual work.

The ones worth uploading:

- Your logo and a cover photo
- Your team, including you
- Your truck or storefront (people look for the van they'll see in their driveway)
- Before-and-after shots of finished jobs
- The inside of your shop or dining room
- Your crew actually working

A deck builder with thirty photos of finished decks will beat a competitor with two blurry pictures every time, even if the competitor is technically closer. Take five minutes at the end of a job and photograph the work. Upload a few every month. Steady activity on the profile is itself a signal.

One practical note: shoot photos on your phone at the job site with location services on. Don't strip metadata, don't run them through a filter app. Just upload them. When you want a stronger foundation — cover photo, team shots, truck shots, and short video for the profile — a [professional business photography and video shoot](/blog/business-photography-near-me-houston/) fills the gaps faster than waiting for enough phone pics to accumulate.

## Step 5: Reviews are the engine

Among the things you can control, reviews move the needle hardest. Quantity, average rating, recency, and whether you reply all factor in.

A few things that work:

**Ask every single customer.** Not the ones you think loved you — everyone. Most happy customers never think to leave a review unless asked.

**Ask at the right moment.** For a trade, that's while you're standing in the driveway after the job and they're happy. Not three days later by email. Say something like: "If you were happy with how this went, a quick Google review would really help us out — I'll text you the link right now."

**Make it one tap.** Google gives every profile a short review link. Get it from your Business Profile dashboard, save it as a text shortcut on your phone, and send it while you're still there. Every extra step loses people.

**Reply to all of them.** Good and bad. A short thank-you on the good ones. On the bad ones, respond calmly, take it offline, and don't argue. Prospects read your replies to the angry reviews more carefully than anything else on the page. A measured response to an unfair one-star review sells better than five glowing ones.

**Never buy reviews or ask your cousins.** Google is decent at catching review fraud and the penalty is losing the whole listing. Not worth it.

If reviews mentioning specific services keep appearing — "they replaced our water heater same day" — that helps Google connect you to those searches. Nudge customers by asking them to mention what you did for them.

## Step 6: Use the parts of the profile most people ignore

**Posts.** You can publish short updates with a photo — seasonal offers, a job you just finished, a note that you're booking two weeks out. They show on your profile and they signal to Google that the listing is actively managed. Once or twice a month is plenty.

**Q&A.** Anyone can ask a question on your profile, and anyone can answer — including competitors and confused strangers. Get ahead of it: post your own common questions and answer them yourself. "Do you offer free estimates?" "What areas do you serve?" "Do you work weekends?" Turn on notifications so you catch real questions fast.

**Messaging.** Lets customers text you through the profile. Only turn it on if you'll actually answer quickly. A slow reply is worse than no messaging button.

**Booking link.** If you take online appointments — salons, mobile detailers, dog groomers — connect it. Removing a phone call from the process converts better than you'd expect.

## Step 7: You still need a website

A Business Profile gets you into the map. A website gets you into the organic results, and it's a large part of how Google decides whether to trust the profile in the first place.

Think of it this way: Google is trying to figure out whether you're a real business that can handle the job. Your profile says you're a roofer. Your website — with your service pages, your photos, your license number, your service area, your phone number matching the profile — is the corroborating evidence.

What a small local site actually needs:

- **A page for each main service.** Not one "Services" page with a bulleted list. A separate page for roof replacement, another for repairs, another for gutters. Each one is a shot at ranking for that search.
- **A page for each major town you serve**, if you cover multiple areas — with genuinely different content on each, not the same paragraph with the town name swapped.
- **Your phone number visible at the top of every page**, tappable on a phone.
- **Fast loading.** Most of your traffic is on a phone, often on cell data in a driveway. A site that takes six seconds loses them.
- **Your NAP** — name, address, phone — in the footer, matching your Business Profile exactly.
- **Real photos of your work,** same as the profile.

More than half of local searches happen on a phone. If your site is hard to use with a thumb, none of the rest matters.

## Step 8: Consistency across the web

Google cross-references your business information against other places it appears online — Yelp, Facebook, Angi, the BBB, industry directories, your chamber of commerce. When those all agree, Google gets more confident you're legitimate. When your old address is still floating around on four directories, that confidence drops.

So: pick one exact format for your name, address, and phone number and use it everywhere. "Suite 4" vs "Ste 4" isn't worth losing sleep over, but a wrong phone number or an old address absolutely is.

The cleanup worth doing:

1. Google your business name and phone number and see what comes up.
2. Fix or claim anything with outdated info.
3. Get listed on the obvious ones for your trade — Yelp, Facebook, Nextdoor, Angi or Houzz for home services, your local chamber.
4. Don't pay for a service that blasts you into 500 junk directories. It doesn't help and it creates messes you'll clean up later.

## How long does any of this take to work?

Verification: days to a couple of weeks. Appearing in the map pack at all: usually shortly after verification. Ranking well for competitive searches: months, not weeks.

In a small town with three competitors, you might be in the top three within a month or two of doing this properly. In a metro area with two hundred plumbers, expect six to twelve months of steady work — reviews coming in, photos going up, website pages getting built — before you're consistently in the pack for the big searches.

Anyone promising page one in thirty days is selling something.

## What it costs

The Google Business Profile itself is free and always has been. If someone calls claiming you need to pay Google to stay listed, it's a scam — and those calls are constant once you get verified.

What does cost money:

- **A website.** Driven mostly by how many pages you need, whether you need booking or e-commerce, and whether someone's writing the content or you are.
- **Ongoing SEO or profile management,** if you don't want to do it yourself.
- **Ads,** if you go that route. Budget is entirely yours to set, but competitive trades in big markets pay real money per click.

The honest math: the free stuff — a complete profile, consistent reviews, good photos — gets most small local businesses most of the way. Paid work is about compounding that, not replacing it.

## The mistakes that cost people the most

**Keyword-stuffing the business name.** Suspension risk, and it's the number one thing competitors report.

**Fake or borrowed addresses.** A UPS box or your buddy's shop across town to fake a second location. Google is very good at catching this now.

**Duplicate listings.** Merge or remove them. Split reviews help nobody.

**Letting the profile go stale.** No new photos, no posts, no review replies for a year. Activity matters.

**Wrong or unanswered phone number.** All the ranking in the world is worthless if the calls go to voicemail. Answer the phone or pay someone to.

**Ignoring one bad review.** One unanswered one-star sitting at the top of your profile does more damage than most owners realize.

**Losing account access.** Know who owns the Google account tied to your profile. If it's a former employee or an agency you've parted ways with, get ownership transferred now, not when you need it.

## A maintenance routine that actually fits a working week

You don't need to do this daily. Try this:

**Weekly, ten minutes.** Reply to new reviews. Check for new Q&A. Answer any messages.

**Monthly, thirty minutes.** Upload five to ten job photos. Post one update. Check your profile insights — how many calls, how many direction requests, what people searched to find you.

**Quarterly.** Confirm your hours, services, and service area are still right. Check that your website and profile still match. Look at what your top competitors are doing differently.

That's it. Consistent small effort beats a big push followed by two years of silence.

## Putting it in order

If you're starting from nothing, do it in this sequence:

1. Claim and verify your Google Business Profile.
2. Fill out every field — category, services, hours, description, service area.
3. Upload twenty photos of real work.
4. Start asking every customer for a review, with a link, on the spot.
5. Get a real website up with a page per service.
6. Clean up your listings elsewhere so everything matches.
7. Keep at it monthly.
8. Only then think about ads.

Each step makes the next one work better. Ads pointed at a weak website waste money. A great website with no profile misses the map pack. It's a system.
