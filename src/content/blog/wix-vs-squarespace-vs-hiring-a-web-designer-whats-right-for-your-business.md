## The short answer

For most small local businesses, **Squarespace** is the easiest builder to get a decent-looking site live on your own, **Wix** gives you the most flexibility if you're willing to fuss with it, and **WordPress** is what you want if the site needs to grow into something bigger. But the builder matters far less than whether anyone actually finishes the site, keeps it updated, and sets it up so Google can find it. Most small business sites fail on that part, not on the software.

That's the honest version. Now let's go deeper, because "which builder" is usually the wrong first question.

## The question you should actually be asking

Every builder on the market can produce a perfectly good website for a plumbing company, a salon, or a two-truck landscaping outfit. The technology stopped being the bottleneck years ago.

The real variables are:

- How many hours do you have, realistically, over the next month?
- Who's going to update it when you add a service, change your hours, or hire a second crew?
- Does this site need to make the phone ring, or does it just need to exist so people can check that you're real?

That last one matters more than people think. A site that just needs to exist — a card you can point to — is a weekend project. A site that needs to show up when someone searches "emergency water heater repair near me" at 11pm is a different animal, and the builder you pick is maybe 10% of the work.

## The main options, straight

### Squarespace

**Good for:** Restaurants, salons, photographers, interior designers, anyone whose business sells on looking good.

Squarespace templates are the best-looking out of the box, full stop. You pick one, swap the photos and text, and you have something that doesn't embarrass you. The editor is restrictive on purpose — you can't easily make it ugly, which for most owners is a feature.

**Where it bites you:** The restriction cuts both ways. If you want a specific layout — say, a service area page for each of the eleven towns you cover, each one built the same way — you'll be doing a lot of copy-paste. Squarespace's local SEO tools are passable but thin. And the blogging side is fine but not great if you plan to publish regularly.

### Wix

**Good for:** People who want control and don't mind clicking around.

Wix lets you drag anything anywhere. That's genuinely useful when you need a layout nobody's template anticipated. It has a decent built-in booking system, which matters if you're a salon, a mobile detailer, or a dog groomer taking appointments.

**Where it bites you:** Freedom to drag anything anywhere also means freedom to build something that looks fine on your laptop and falls apart on a phone. Wix has gotten much better at this, but it still happens, and about three-quarters of your visitors are on a phone. Also, Wix sites tend to accumulate plugins and apps that slow the whole thing down.

### WordPress

**Good for:** Businesses that will outgrow a brochure site — multi-location, lots of service pages, real content strategy, or anything custom.

WordPress runs a huge chunk of the web for a reason. You own the site outright. You can move it to a different host whenever you like. There's a plugin for everything, including the best SEO tooling available anywhere.

**Where it bites you:** It's not a weekend DIY project for most owners. You're dealing with hosting, themes, plugins, updates, and security. When something breaks — and something will break — you need to know what to do or know who to call. Plenty of small business owners start a WordPress site and abandon it half-built.

Worth noting: WordPress.com (the hosted service) and WordPress.org (the software you install) are different things with the same name, which is confusing and always has been.

### GoDaddy, Web.com, and the host-bundled builders

**Good for:** Getting something live fast when the alternative is nothing.

These are usually bundled with a domain you already bought. They're quick. The AI-generated starter sites they spit out are better than they used to be.

**Where it bites you:** They're generic. If you've seen one GoDaddy site for a contractor, you've seen all of them, and so has your customer. They're also the hardest to leave — the content doesn't export cleanly, so switching later means rebuilding from scratch.

### Shopify

**Good for:** Selling physical products online. That's it.

If you're a bakery shipping cookies or a shop selling parts, Shopify is the right tool and nothing else is close. If you're a roofer, it's the wrong tool, even though plenty of people have been talked into it.

### Webflow and Framer

**Good for:** Designers. Not you.

These produce excellent, fast sites, but the learning curve is steep enough that it's genuinely a profession. If you're hiring someone and they say they build in Webflow, that's fine. If you're doing it yourself, skip it.

### Google Business Profile with no website

I'll say the quiet part out loud: a well-filled-out Google Business Profile with real photos and a steady drip of reviews will out-perform a bad website for a lot of local trades. If you've got zero hours and zero budget, do that first.

But it's not a substitute. You don't own it, Google can suspend it, and you can't control what it says about you. Treat it as the front door, not the house.

## What actually matters for a local business site

Here's the part nobody selling you a builder talks about. Whichever platform you land on, these are the things that decide whether the site earns its keep.

### Speed on a phone, on mobile data

Your customer is standing in a flooded basement with one bar of signal. If your homepage takes eight seconds because it's loading a hero video and six tracking scripts, they're gone. Test your own site on your phone, off wifi, in a parking lot. Most owners have never done this.

### A phone number that's a tappable link

Sounds stupid. Isn't. A huge number of small business sites have the phone number as part of an image or as plain text that doesn't dial when tapped. Every builder supports this correctly; people just don't set it up.

### One page per service, one page per area

This is the single biggest difference between a site that gets found and one that doesn't. If you do drain cleaning, water heaters, repiping, and sewer line work, that's four pages — not four bullet points on one "Services" page. If you cover six towns, that's six pages, each one actually written about that town, not copy-pasted with the name swapped.

This is grinding work. It's also the work. Any builder can do it; the question is whether you will.

### Real photos of your actual work

Stock photos of a smiling guy in a clean uniform fool nobody. Photos of your truck, your crew, the deck you finished last Tuesday — that's what converts. Take them with your phone. They don't have to be perfect.

### Reviews on the page

Pull your Google reviews onto the site. Most builders have a widget or a plugin for this. It's one of the cheapest trust-builders available.

### Forms that actually reach you

I've audited sites where the contact form had been dumping into a dead inbox for two years. The owner thought business was slow. Test your form after you build it, and test it again every few months.

## What DIY really costs you

Nobody quotes you the true price of doing it yourself, so here it is in the units that matter: hours.

A genuinely simple five-page site, built by a non-technical owner on Squarespace or Wix, writing their own copy, is a solid two to four weekends. That assumes you don't get precious about it. Most people do get precious about it, and the project stretches into month three, and by then the enthusiasm's gone and it sits at 80% done.

The subscription fee is the small number. Your Saturday is the big one. If you bill out your time on a job, run that math before you decide DIY is the cheap option.

What drives DIY cost up:
- Needing e-commerce or online booking (more expensive plan tiers, more setup)
- Buying premium templates or apps to fill gaps
- Custom email addresses — usually a separate subscription
- Redoing it in a year when you realize it isn't working

That last one is the expensive mistake. Plenty of businesses pay for a DIY site, abandon it, and then pay a pro — so they've paid twice.

## What hiring a pro actually gets you

The deliverable isn't "a website." Anyone can make a website. What you're buying is:

- **Decisions made for you.** Someone who's built a hundred contractor sites knows what the homepage needs to say and in what order. You don't spend three weeks agonizing over whether to lead with the phone number or the testimonial.
- **Copy that's written.** Writing about your own business is harder than it sounds. Most DIY sites stall right here.
- **The technical setup done correctly.** SSL, mobile, page speed, structured data, Google Search Console, analytics, the sitemap. None of it is glamorous. All of it matters.
- **Someone to call.** When the site goes down at 6am or you need the holiday hours changed, you text someone instead of googling "why is my Wix site blank."

What drives the cost of a professional build up: lots of pages, custom functionality like booking or quoting tools, e-commerce, multiple locations, copywriting from scratch, and photography. What keeps it down: a clear page count, your own photos, and knowing what you want to say.

What's usually included either way: hosting, domain setup, the design, mobile layout, and basic SEO groundwork. If a quote doesn't mention those, ask.

## So which should you do?

**Build it yourself if:** you have genuine free time, the site mostly needs to exist and look legitimate, your business is simple to explain, and you enjoy this kind of project. A good DIY site beats no site by a mile.

**Hire someone if:** the phone ringing is the whole point, you're in a competitive trade where everyone's bidding on the same searches, you have more than a handful of services or service areas, or — this is the big one — you've already tried DIY and it's been sitting half-finished for two months.

There's no shame in either. You wouldn't let a homeowner re-pipe their own bathroom to save a few hours of your time, and you'd be right. Same logic applies in reverse.

## The lock-in question

Before you commit, ask: how hard is it to leave?

- **WordPress:** easy. You own the files and the database. Move hosts whenever.
- **Squarespace and Wix:** your domain is portable, your content mostly isn't. Expect to rebuild.
- **GoDaddy-style bundled builders:** hardest. Plan on starting over.

And whatever you do, **register the domain in your own name, with your own account.** Not your nephew's. Not the agency's. Yours. The number of businesses who've lost control of their own domain because the guy who set it up stopped answering the phone is genuinely depressing. If you take one thing from this post, take that.

## A short checklist before you pick

1. Count your pages. Services plus service areas plus about, contact, and home.
2. Decide if you need booking, quoting, or payments. That narrows things fast.
3. Be honest about your hours over the next 30 days.
4. Check that whatever you pick lets you own your domain and export your content.
5. Pick one and start. A live, imperfect site beats a perfect plan.

The builder you choose matters less than finishing. A plain Squarespace site with four service pages, real photos, and a tappable phone number will out-earn a gorgeous half-built Webflow site every single day.
