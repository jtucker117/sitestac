## The short answer

Getting more customers online comes down to three things: showing up where people are searching (mostly Google and Google Maps), giving them a website that answers their question and makes contact easy, and following up fast when they reach out. Everything else — social media, ads, fancy video — is a multiplier on top of those three. If the basics are broken, nothing else you spend money on will stick.

Most local businesses I talk to don't have a traffic problem. They have a leak problem. People are already finding them and then bouncing because the phone number isn't clickable on a phone, or the site loads in eight seconds, or there's no way to tell whether the company is still in business.

Let's walk through the whole thing in order.

## First, understand how a local customer actually buys

Picture a woman whose water heater just started leaking into the garage. Here's what happens in the next 20 minutes:

1. She grabs her phone and searches something like "water heater repair near me."
2. She looks at the map results — the three businesses in the box at the top — and skims the star ratings.
3. She taps two or three of them. Maybe she looks at a website. Mostly she's looking for: are you close, do you do this exact thing, and do other people say you're not a disaster.
4. She calls the first one or two that look legitimate.
5. Whoever answers the phone gets the job.

That's it. That's the whole funnel for most local service work. No one is reading your blog for a week and subscribing to your newsletter before they call about a leaking water heater.

So your online strategy isn't really "marketing" in the ad-agency sense. It's making sure you're in the list, you look credible, and you're easy to contact. A restaurant or salon looks slightly different — more browsing, more photos, more "is this place my style" — but the shape is the same.

## Step 1: Own your Google Business Profile

If you do one thing this month, do this. Your Google Business Profile is the free listing that puts you in the map pack and on the right side of search results. For most local businesses it drives more calls than the website does.

What to get right:

**Categories.** Pick the primary category that matches what you most want to be called for. A guy who does both remodels and handyman work has to choose which one he wants the phone to ring about. Add secondary categories for the rest.

**Service area or address.** If customers come to you — salon, restaurant, shop — use the real address and make sure it's exact. If you go to them, set a service area. Don't fake an address in a town you don't operate in; Google is good at catching it and it can get your listing suspended.

**Hours, including holidays.** Wrong hours is the fastest way to lose a customer who's standing outside your door.

**Photos.** Real ones. Your truck, your crew, your shop, finished jobs. Not stock photos of a smiling model holding a wrench. Add new ones regularly — it signals the listing is active.

**Services and descriptions.** List out the individual things you do: "tankless water heater installation," "sewer camera inspection," "panel upgrade." These get matched against what people search.

**Q&A and posts.** You can answer your own common questions in the Q&A section. Do it. "Do you charge for estimates?" "Do you work weekends?" Those questions decide whether someone calls you or the next guy.

The businesses that win the map pack are usually the ones that treat the profile like a living thing instead of something they set up once in 2019.

## Step 2: Make your website answer the question, fast

Once someone taps through to your site, you have a few seconds. Here's what has to be obvious without scrolling:

- What you do
- Where you do it
- How to reach you

That sounds stupidly basic until you look at most small business sites. The top of the page says "Welcome to Our Website" over a photo of a sunset, and the phone number is in an image in the footer.

For a roofer in Boise, the top of the homepage should say something close to: "Roof repair and replacement in Boise and Meridian" with a big tap-to-call button and a short form. That's it. Everything else can come after.

Other things that matter more than people think:

**Speed.** If your site takes more than about three seconds to load on a phone on a regular cell connection, you're losing people. Huge uncompressed photos are the usual culprit.

**Mobile.** Most local searches happen on a phone. Buttons need to be thumb-sized. Text needs to be readable without pinching. If you have to zoom to read your own site, it's costing you jobs.

**Tap-to-call.** Your phone number should be a link that dials when tapped, in the header, on every page. Not a graphic. Not just in the footer.

**A page for each real service.** One page called "Services" with a bulleted list of 14 things doesn't rank for any of them and doesn't convince anyone. Separate pages — "Drain Cleaning," "Water Heater Replacement," "Repipes" — give Google something to match and give the customer confidence you actually do that work.

**Pages for the towns you serve.** If you cover five towns, a real page for each one, with actual jobs you've done there, beats one page that lists town names in a paragraph. Real content, not the same page copied five times with the name swapped.

## Step 3: Plug the leaks between visit and phone call

This is where most of the money is, and it's the cheapest thing to fix.

**Cut the form down.** Name, phone, and a short "what's going on" box. Every extra field costs you submissions. You don't need their street address before they've even talked to you.

**Offer more than one way to reach you.** Some people call. Some will never call — they'll text or fill out a form at 11 p.m. Give them both. Text-enabled business numbers convert really well for trades.

**Be clear about how you charge.** You don't have to publish prices. But "free estimates on replacements, diagnostic fee for repairs" saves you both a lot of time and makes you look honest. Vagueness reads as expensive.

**Show proof above the fold or close to it.** Star rating, number of reviews, years in business, license number, insurance. A stranger is about to let you into their house.

**Make the next step obvious on every page.** Every page should end in a call or form. Don't make someone hunt back to the homepage.

I've watched sites double their lead count without a single new visitor, just from fixing those five things.

## Step 4: Get reviews on purpose

Reviews do two jobs. They help you rank in the map pack, and they're the single biggest reason someone picks you over the shop next door. Volume matters, recency matters, and how you respond matters.

The only reliable system I've seen is this: ask every customer, at the moment they're happiest, with a link that takes one tap.

For a plumber, that's right after the water's running again and the customer says "oh thank god." Not three weeks later in an email blast. Have a short link saved in your phone, text it while you're still in the driveway.

A few rules:

- Don't offer anything in exchange. Against Google's rules and it shows in the reviews.
- Respond to every one, good and bad. Short and human. "Thanks Karen, glad we got the AC back up before the weekend."
- Answer bad reviews calmly and factually. Future customers read your response more closely than the complaint. A defensive owner scares people off worse than a one-star review does.
- Don't buy them. It's obvious, and a cluster of five-star reviews from accounts with no history looks worse than nothing.

Also worth having a presence where your industry's customers look: Yelp for restaurants, Angi or Thumbtack for some trades, Facebook for local recommendations. But Google is the one that moves the needle.

## Step 5: Show up for the searches that actually pay

Not all search traffic is worth the same. "How does a heat pump work" brings students and tire-kickers. "Heat pump replacement cost Tucson" brings buyers.

Focus on the phrases that contain intent to hire:

- Service + city ("electrician Chandler AZ")
- Service + "near me"
- Emergency phrasing ("24 hour plumber," "same day AC repair")
- Brand and model work ("Trane dealer," "Sub-Zero repair")
- Specific problems ("breaker keeps tripping," "tile shower leaking")

Each of those deserves its own page or at least its own section with real language on it. Write it the way you'd explain it standing in a customer's kitchen. Google has gotten good at recognizing genuine expertise and equally good at ignoring keyword soup.

This is also where a blog earns its keep, but only if it's answering questions you actually get asked. "Why is my furnace blowing cold air" is a great post because the person searching it has a problem right now and lives somewhere. "Top 10 HVAC trends of 2026" is a waste of your evening.

## Step 6: Show your work

Proof beats claims every time. "Quality workmanship since 1998" means nothing. Thirty before-and-after photos of your actual jobs means everything.

Take pictures on every job. It takes 20 seconds. Get the before, get the after, get one of the crew working. Then:

- Put them on service pages next to the relevant work
- Put them on your Google Business Profile
- Build a simple gallery organized by type of job, not by date

For a restaurant, this is the food and the room. For a salon, it's the work — and hair and nail clients will scroll a hundred photos before booking. For a landscaper, it's the transformation shots. Same principle: strangers deciding whether to trust you want to see evidence, not adjectives.

Add short written details next to the photos. "Full re-roof, 1978 ranch in Kuna, tore off two layers, GAF Timberline, done in two days." That one sentence does more selling than a page of copy.

## Step 7: Answer fast, or it doesn't matter

The business that responds first usually wins. This is the least glamorous and highest-return thing on this list.

If someone submits a form at 2 p.m. and hears back at 6 p.m., they've already called two competitors. If you can't answer the phone while you're on a ladder, you need something covering it — an answering service, a dispatcher, your spouse, a shared inbox someone actually watches.

Set a simple rule: every inquiry gets a human response within an hour during business hours, and first thing in the morning if it came in overnight. Even "Got your message, I'll call you at 4 with a time" keeps the job alive.

And track it. Most owners think they answer fast. Look at the timestamps on the last 20 leads and you'll usually find a few that never got a reply at all.

## Where paid ads fit

Ads are an accelerator, not a foundation. If your site doesn't convert and you have nine reviews, paid clicks just let you lose money faster.

Once the basics are solid, they're useful in a few situations:

**You're new or just moved into a new area.** Ranking organically takes months. Ads work today.

**You have a seasonal window.** AC repair in July, gutter cleaning in October, holiday catering. Turn them on for the season and off after.

**You want a specific high-value job.** You'd rather do full bath remodels than clogged drains — you can bid on the phrasing for the work you want.

What drives ad cost up is competition and how broad you go. Bidding on "plumber" in a big metro against private-equity-backed shops with big budgets is a grinder. Bidding on "tankless water heater installation" plus your suburb is much more manageable. Tight targeting, tight service area, phone-call-focused campaigns.

Google's Local Services Ads (the ones with the green "Google Guaranteed" badge) are usually a better starting point for trades than regular search ads. You pay per lead instead of per click, and you sit above everything else.

Whatever you run, insist on knowing what a booked job costs you, not what a click costs. If your ad person can't tell you that, they're not measuring the right thing.

## Where social media fits

Honest take: social media rarely generates emergency service calls. Nobody's browsing Instagram when the toilet's overflowing.

It's genuinely valuable for:

- **Restaurants, salons, bakeries, gyms** — anything where the decision is visual and the purchase is discretionary. Here it's a primary channel, not a side project.
- **Staying visible to past customers** so you're the name they remember in two years.
- **Big-ticket visual trades** — remodels, landscaping, custom cabinetry — where people spend months gathering ideas.
- **Local Facebook groups.** The "anyone know a good electrician?" post is still one of the strongest lead sources in most towns. Being a real, helpful participant there beats any ad.

If you're a service business with limited time, don't try to feed five platforms. Pick one, post your actual work a couple times a week, and put the rest of your energy into search and reviews.

## Don't forget the customers you already have

You already have a list of people who paid you and liked the work. That's the cheapest source of business you'll ever have and almost nobody uses it.

Simple things that work:

- A text or email at service-interval time. "Hey, it's been a year since we serviced your furnace — want to get on the schedule before the cold hits?"
- A quick note after a big job with a couple of related things you do, because half your customers have no idea you also do panel upgrades or generators.
- A referral ask. Not a whole program with punch cards. Just "if you know anyone who needs this, pass my number along."

A hair salon texting last month's clients to fill Tuesday openings is doing more effective online marketing than most businesses running ads.

## What to actually measure

Skip the vanity numbers. Page views and follower counts don't pay anybody. Track four things:

1. **Calls and form submissions per month.** The top of your funnel. Use call tracking or at minimum a separate number on the website so you know what came from where.
2. **How many turned into booked jobs.** If you're getting plenty of leads and booking few, the problem is your intake or your pricing, not your marketing.
3. **Where they came from.** Google Maps, Google search, ads, referral, Facebook. You need to know which channel to feed.
4. **Review count and rating over time.** Should go up every month. If it's flat, your asking system isn't running.

Look at it monthly. Not daily — local numbers are noisy week to week and you'll make bad decisions chasing noise.

## A realistic 90-day plan

If you're starting from scratch, do it in this order. Don't do it all at once.

**Weeks 1–2:** Claim and fully fill out your Google Business Profile. Categories, hours, services, 20 real photos. Check that your name, address and phone match everywhere online.

**Weeks 3–4:** Fix the website basics. Clear headline with what you do and where, tap-to-call in the header, short form, fast-loading images, readable on a phone.

**Weeks 5–6:** Build out a page for each core service and each main town you serve. Real content on each one.

**Weeks 7–8:** Put a review system in place. Short link, ask on every job, respond to everything that comes in.

**Weeks 9–10:** Set up tracking so you know where leads come from, and tighten your response time.

**Weeks 11–12:** Now consider ads, and only in the areas or services where you want more work.

Three months of that puts you ahead of most of your competition, because most of your competition set up a website once and never touched it again.

## The mistakes that cost the most

**Chasing traffic instead of fixing conversion.** More visitors to a broken site is just more wasted visitors.

**Redesigning instead of repairing.** Plenty of sites need three fixes, not a rebuild. Know which one you're dealing with.

**No clear service area.** "We serve the greater metro area" tells Google and customers nothing. Name the towns.

**Hiding behind a contact form.** Some people will only call. Some will only text. Offer everything.

**Letting leads sit.** The slowest responder loses, every time.

**Doing it once.** Your Google profile, your photos, your reviews — these are ongoing, like maintaining equipment. A little every month beats a big push once a year.

None of this is complicated. It's just unglamorous, and it takes someone actually doing it. If you nail the map listing, a site that converts, a steady flow of reviews and a fast response, you'll outperform competitors spending far more than you.
