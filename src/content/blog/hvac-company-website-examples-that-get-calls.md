## The short answer

HVAC websites that get calls all do the same handful of things: phone number visible at the top on mobile, a clear line about what you fix and where, real photos of your trucks and techs, and an emergency/same-day option spelled out. Everything else — sliders, mission statements, stock photos of smiling families — is decoration. If a homeowner with a dead furnace at 9pm can find your number in under three seconds, you've got a site that works.

Below is what that looks like in practice, broken down by the pieces you can copy.

## What a call-getting HVAC site looks like above the fold

Picture a homeowner in July. AC quit. They're hot, annoyed, and searching on a phone. They will open two or three sites and call whichever one makes it easiest.

The sites that win that call have, in the top screen:

- The company name and the town or metro. "Ridgeline Heating & Air — Serving Boise and Meridian."
- A tappable phone number. Not an image of a number. Not buried in a hamburger menu.
- One sentence about urgency: "Same-day AC repair. We answer nights and weekends."
- A photo of an actual van or an actual tech, not a stock model in a pristine white polo.

That's it. No carousel. No paragraph about your commitment to excellence. The homeowner isn't reading — they're scanning for a reason to trust you and a way to dial.

One HVAC contractor we've seen do this well put a simple bar across the top of every page: phone number on the left, "Emergency Service" button on the right. On mobile it sticks to the bottom of the screen as you scroll. You literally cannot be on the site without the phone number in view.

## Separate pages for separate problems

Here's the mistake most HVAC sites make: one page called "Services" with a bulleted list of fifteen things.

The sites that pull calls from Google build a real page for each big service:

- AC repair
- AC installation and replacement
- Furnace repair
- Furnace installation
- Heat pump service
- Ductwork and indoor air quality
- Maintenance plans

Why it matters: somebody searching "furnace blowing cold air" is a different customer than somebody searching "heat pump replacement cost." The first is an emergency repair call tonight. The second is a quote you'll close next week. A single Services page tries to speak to both and lands with neither.

Each page should read like you'd talk on the phone. What are the usual causes. What you check. How fast you can get out. Whether you carry common parts on the truck. If you service a specific brand a lot in your area — Trane, Carrier, whoever the local builders installed — say so.

This is the same logic behind [what a plumbing company website needs](/blog/website-for-plumbing-company-what-it-needs/), and it holds for any trade where customers search by symptom instead of by service name.

## Service area pages that name actual towns

HVAC is geographic. You'll drive 25 minutes, not 90. Your site should say so in plain language.

The good examples have a page per town or a page per cluster of towns, and those pages aren't copy-paste with the name swapped. They mention real things: the older neighborhoods with boilers, the new subdivision where everything's a heat pump, the county that requires a permit for a changeout.

A homeowner in a smaller suburb outside your main city wants to know you actually come out there. Naming their town on the page is the cheapest reassurance you can offer.

## Photos of your work, not a photo library

Stock photos hurt HVAC sites more than most trades, because the customer is about to let a stranger into their house and their crawlspace.

The sites that get calls show:

- The van with the wrap and the plate
- Techs in branded shirts, on a real roof or in a real attic
- Before and after on a condenser pad or a nasty return duct
- The owner, with a name

You don't need a photographer. A tech taking three phone photos a week on jobs — with the customer's okay — builds a library fast. Blurry-but-real beats crisp-but-fake every single time.

## Reviews where the decision gets made

Most HVAC sites dump testimonials on a "Reviews" page nobody visits. The ones that convert put two or three short reviews right on the service page, near the call button.

Better still if the review matches the page. On the AC repair page, use the review from the woman who said you showed up at 7pm on a Saturday. On the installation page, use the one about the clean job site and the walkthrough of the new thermostat.

Specific beats glowing. "Great service, highly recommend" convinces nobody. "Diagnosed a bad capacitor in ten minutes and had one on the truck" convinces everybody.

## Forms that don't ask for a novel

If you're going to have a contact form, keep it to name, phone, and "what's going on." Every extra field costs you submissions. Asking for a preferred appointment window, a full address, and the model number of their unit before you've even spoken is how you lose the job to the guy with three fields.

And if you offer financing or maintenance plans, say so in a line near the form. Price anxiety is the number one reason a homeowner closes the tab on a replacement quote.

## The boring stuff that quietly kills calls

A lot of HVAC sites look fine and still don't ring. Usually it's one of these:

**Slow on mobile.** A big background video and eight uncompressed photos means a three-second wait on a phone in a driveway. People bail.

**Phone number that isn't tappable.** If it's inside an image or typed as plain text without a tel link, half your visitors won't dial.

**No hours, or wrong hours.** If you take after-hours calls, say it. If you don't, say that too — otherwise you get angry voicemails at midnight.

**Mismatched info.** Your Google Business Profile says one phone number, the site says another. Google notices, customers notice, and your map ranking suffers.

**Dead seasonal content.** A banner about spring AC tune-ups still running in November tells people nobody's minding the store.

These are the same fundamentals covered in [website essentials for local service businesses](/blog/website-essentials-for-local-service-businesses/) — HVAC just punishes you harder for getting them wrong, because your customer is usually uncomfortable and in a hurry.

## What drives the cost of building one

A straightforward HVAC site — home page, five or six service pages, a few service area pages, photos, and a contact form — is a modest project. What pushes it up: online scheduling that syncs to your dispatch software, a customer portal for maintenance plan members, a large number of city pages, or custom quote calculators.

What usually pushes it up for no return: animation, a blog nobody writes, and a redesign every two years. Spend on the pages that answer questions and the photos that prove you're real.

## Copy the pattern, not the design

The best HVAC websites in your market probably aren't the prettiest ones. Go look at three competitors on your phone. Time how long it takes to find a phone number. See whether they name your town. See whether the photos are real.

Then build yours to beat them on those three things. That's the whole game.
