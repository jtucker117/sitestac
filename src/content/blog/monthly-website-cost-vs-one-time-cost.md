## The short answer

A one-time cost buys you a website. A monthly cost buys you a website plus somebody who keeps it running. Neither one is a trick — the trouble starts when you pay a one-time fee, assume you're done, and then find out hosting, updates, and edits were never part of the deal.

Most small local businesses are better off with a monthly arrangement, because a website that nobody touches for three years stops pulling its weight. But there are real cases where paying once and owning it outright makes more sense, and I'll get into those.

## What "one-time cost" actually covers

When a designer quotes you a single project fee, you're usually paying for: the design, the build, the pages, and getting it live. That's it. That's the deliverable.

What's almost never in that number:

- **Hosting.** The server your site sits on. Someone has to pay for it every month, and if it's not in your quote, that someone is you.
- **The domain.** Renews yearly, forever.
- **Software updates.** If your site runs on WordPress, the platform and its plugins push updates constantly. Skip them long enough and something breaks or gets hacked.
- **Edits.** New service area, new phone number, you dropped a service line, your hours changed for winter. Every one of those is a separate call and a separate invoice.
- **Backups and security.** Boring until the day it isn't.

I've seen this play out plenty. A roofer pays a lump sum for a nice site in the spring. Eighteen months later he wants to add a photo gallery of a big commercial job. The designer has moved on, the site is running an old version of everything, and now it's a rebuild instead of an edit.

That's the hidden cost of one-time. Not the invoice — the drift.

## What "monthly" actually covers

A monthly website is closer to a service than a product. You're paying for the site to exist, stay online, stay current, and stay fixable.

A fair monthly arrangement should include hosting, the SSL certificate, security and backups, software updates, and a reasonable number of content edits. Some include basic SEO upkeep and reporting on how many people called you from the site.

The advantages are pretty practical:

- **No big hit up front.** If you're a two-year-old landscaping outfit, a large lump sum in March hurts more than a small line item every month.
- **One number to call.** Site's down, form's not sending, Google's showing your old address — you call one person and it's covered.
- **It stays current.** Somebody's actually looking at it.

The downside: you're paying as long as you have the site. Stop paying and, depending on the agreement, the site may come down. That's the part worth reading carefully before you sign anything.

## The question that matters more than the pricing model

Ask this: **if I stop paying, what do I keep?**

Good answers:
- You own your domain name. Always. It should be registered in your name, in your account. Non-negotiable.
- You own your content — your photos, your copy, your reviews.
- You can get an export or a copy of the site to move elsewhere.

Bad answers:
- The domain is registered to the agency.
- "The site is on our proprietary platform, it doesn't transfer."
- Nobody will give you a straight answer.

That last one is the real red flag. A monthly model isn't a lock-in by itself. A monthly model where you can't leave is. Plenty of reputable shops run monthly and will hand you your stuff on the way out the door — that's how it should work.

## When one-time makes sense

A lump-sum build is a good fit if:

- **You've got somebody in-house who's comfortable with tech.** Your office manager likes this stuff and will keep on top of it. Great — pay once, host it yourself, let her run it.
- **The site is genuinely static.** A single-location barbershop with three services and a phone number might not touch the site for two years, and that's fine.
- **You've got the cash and prefer owning the asset.** Some owners just don't want another recurring bill. Fair enough.

If you go this route, budget separately for hosting and set a reminder to check the site twice a year. And find out up front what an edit costs later — hourly, minimum charge, turnaround time. Get it in writing while they still want your business.

## When monthly makes sense

Monthly is the better fit if:

- **You need the site to bring in work, not just exist.** Anything that has to rank on Google, collect leads, or keep pace with competitors needs ongoing attention.
- **Your business changes.** New services, new trucks, new locations, seasonal promotions. An HVAC company running maintenance plans in fall and emergency AC in July needs a site that shifts with it.
- **You don't want to think about it.** This is most owners, honestly. You're good at your trade. Website maintenance isn't a skill you need to acquire.
- **Cash flow matters more than total spend.** It usually does when you're growing.

## What drives cost either way

The pricing model isn't what makes a site expensive — the scope is. Page count, custom design versus a proven template, booking systems, e-commerce, and how much writing and photography you need done for you all move the number. We break that down in detail in [what drives website design pricing for small business](/blog/website-design-pricing-small-business/), and if you're still trying to get a feel for the range, start with [how much a website costs for a small business](/blog/how-much-does-a-small-business-website-cost-real-numbers/).

A ten-page site with online booking costs what it costs. Whether you pay it in one lump or spread it out is a cash-flow decision, not a value decision.

## The comparison nobody makes

Here's the thing owners miss. They compare a one-time fee against twelve monthly payments and decide monthly is more expensive over the long haul.

Maybe. But run the real math: one-time fee, plus hosting, plus domain, plus the two or three edit invoices a year, plus the rebuild you'll need in three or four years when the site's out of date and everything is broken. Suddenly the gap narrows a lot.

And that's before you count the jobs you didn't get because your site still listed a service you stopped offering, or the contact form quietly stopped sending emails for five months and nobody noticed.

## How to decide

Ask any provider these five things:

1. What's included every month — specifically?
2. How many edits, and what counts as an edit?
3. Who owns the domain and the content?
4. What happens if I cancel?
5. What's the response time when something breaks?

If you get clear answers to all five, either model can work. If you get vague ones, the pricing structure is the least of your problems.
