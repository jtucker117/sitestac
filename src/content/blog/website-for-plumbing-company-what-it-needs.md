## The short answer

A plumbing company website needs four things above everything else: a phone number that's impossible to miss, a clear list of what you fix, the towns you cover, and proof you're real (reviews, photos, license number). Everything else — blog posts, fancy sliders, a chat bot — is optional until those four are nailed down.

Most plumbing sites fail on the basics. They look fine on a laptop and then take eight seconds to load on a phone in a driveway, which is exactly where the customer is standing when their water heater is leaking.

## Your phone number is the whole point

Plumbing is an emergency trade. When someone's got water coming through a ceiling, they are not reading your "About Our Values" page. They're looking for a number to tap.

So put it in the header, make it a tap-to-call link, and repeat it at the bottom of every page. Not an image of a phone number — an actual link, so a thumb tap starts the call.

If you take after-hours calls, say so next to the number. "24/7 emergency service" earns you the 11pm calls that your competitor with the contact form will never get. If you *don't* take after-hours calls, say that too. You'd rather lose the call than get woken up for a job you won't take.

A short form is still worth having for the non-urgent stuff — water softener quotes, repipe estimates, bathroom remodels. Keep it to name, phone, and "what's going on." Every extra field costs you leads.

## Spell out what you actually do

"Full-service plumbing" tells a homeowner nothing. They're searching for the specific thing that's broken.

List the work in the words customers use:

- Water heater repair and replacement (tank and tankless)
- Drain cleaning and clogged toilets
- Sewer line camera inspection and replacement
- Burst and frozen pipes
- Leak detection
- Garbage disposals
- Sump pumps
- Faucet, sink, and toilet installs
- Repiping
- Gas line work, if you're licensed for it
- Backflow testing and certification

The big money-makers deserve their own page. A water heater page can explain tank versus tankless, how long a replacement takes, what a permit involves, and what warranty you pass along. That page can rank on its own and it answers the questions that would otherwise eat twenty minutes of phone time.

Be just as clear about what you don't do. If you're residential only, say it. If you don't touch septic, say it. Filtering out the wrong calls is as valuable as attracting the right ones.

## Service area, named town by town

Google needs to understand where you work, and so does the homeowner two counties over who's about to waste your time.

List the cities, suburbs, and neighborhoods you cover by name. "Greater metro area" doesn't help anybody. If you charge a trip fee past a certain radius, mention that there's a travel charge for outlying areas — no need to quote a figure, just set the expectation.

If a couple of towns make up most of your work, give them their own pages with real local detail. Mention the older neighborhoods with galvanized supply lines, the subdivision with the notorious slab leaks, the city's permit process. That specificity is what separates a real local page from the fake "plumber in [city]" pages everybody can spot.

## Proof that you're legitimate

You're asking a stranger to let you into their house and cut into their walls. The site has to do some reassuring.

**License and insurance.** Put your license number in the footer. Say you're insured and bonded. In plumbing this is a real differentiator, because plenty of the guys underbidding you aren't.

**Reviews.** Pull your best Google reviews onto the homepage and service pages, with first names and towns. Not "Great service! — A customer." Real names read as real.

**Photos of your own work.** Stock photos of a smiling man with a wrench fool nobody. Take before-and-afters on your phone: the corroded heater next to the new one, the sewer line excavation put back to grade, the clean copper on a repipe. Your trucks, your crew, your vans with the logo. This is the single cheapest upgrade most plumbing sites can make.

**How long you've been at it.** "Serving the east side since 2009" does more work than three paragraphs of mission statement.

## It has to be fast and work on a phone

Most of your traffic is on a phone, often on a weak signal in a basement or a driveway. If your site takes more than a few seconds, they hit back and call the next plumber.

That means no giant uncompressed hero images, no autoplay video, no five tracking scripts you don't use. Buttons big enough for a thumb. Text you can read without pinching. This isn't a design preference — it's the difference between getting the call and not.

## Pricing: be honest without boxing yourself in

Plumbing quotes depend on too many variables to publish a number, and any figure you post becomes an argument on a job site later.

What you *can* do is explain how pricing works. Do you charge a diagnostic fee? Is it waived if they proceed with the repair? Flat rate or time and materials? Do you give written estimates before starting? Do you offer financing on a big replacement?

Answering that on the site heads off the most common phone question and pre-qualifies people who were only ever going to price-shop you.

## What's usually missing

A few things that cost nothing and get skipped constantly:

- **Hours, including emergency hours.** Spell it out.
- **Payment methods.** Cards, checks, financing.
- **A real address or at least a city.** A totally anonymous site looks sketchy.
- **Matching Google Business Profile.** Same name, same phone, same address as the site. Mismatches hurt your map ranking.
- **Warranty terms.** Parts and labor, and for how long.
- **Commercial section, if you do commercial.** A property manager needs different information than a homeowner — response time, invoicing, multiple properties.

## What you can skip

You don't need a blog if you're not going to keep it up. You don't need a slider. You don't need live chat if nobody's watching it. You don't need eight pages of plumbing history.

What you need is a site where a wet, stressed homeowner can figure out in ten seconds that you fix their problem, you work in their town, other people trust you, and here's the number.
