## The short answer

Yes — a website can generate leads, but it doesn't do it just by existing. A site brings in work when people can find it in search, it loads fast on a phone, and it makes calling or messaging you dead simple. Miss any one of those and you've got an online brochure nobody reads.

That's the part most owners don't get told. You pay for a website, it goes live, and then... nothing. Not because websites don't work, but because that one does nothing a customer actually needs.

## What a lead-generating site actually does differently

I've looked at hundreds of small business sites. The ones that produce calls almost always have the same handful of things in place. The ones that don't are usually missing the same handful of things.

**It shows up when someone searches.** If a homeowner types "emergency plumber" plus your town and you're on page three, your site isn't generating anything. Being found is step one and there's no way around it. That means pages built around what people actually search for, a Google Business Profile that's filled out and connected, and real information about the areas you serve.

**It answers the question fast.** Somebody landing on your site has one of three questions: Do you do this job? Do you cover my area? How do I reach you? If they can't answer all three in about ten seconds, they hit back and call the next guy.

**It makes contact stupid easy.** Phone number in the header, tappable on mobile. A short form that doesn't ask for a mailing address. Maybe text messaging, because a lot of people under 40 will not call a stranger. Every extra field you add to a form, fewer people finish it.

**It gives people a reason to trust you.** Photos of your actual work, not stock images of someone else's kitchen. Reviews. A real address or at least a real service area. A face. People hire people.

## Why most small business sites don't generate anything

Here's where the money usually goes wrong.

**It was built to look nice, not to work.** A designer made something pretty, the owner said "looks great," and nobody asked whether a roofing customer in a hurry could find the phone number. Compliments from friends are not leads.

**It's slow.** A site that takes six seconds to load on a phone loses most of its visitors before they see anything. Big uncompressed photos are usually the culprit. A landscaper uploading 40 photos straight off their camera can single-handedly kill their own site's speed.

**It's one page about how long you've been in business.** "Family owned since 1994, committed to quality." Nobody searches for that. They search for "water heater replacement" and "gutter cleaning near me" and "bridal hair stylist." If you don't have a page for the thing they're searching, you don't show up for it.

**Nobody's ever touched it since launch.** Google rewards sites that are alive. A site that hasn't changed since 2019 slowly slides down the results while competitors who add a page here and there climb past.

**It was never connected to anything.** No Google Business Profile link, no reviews pulling in, no tracking so you have no idea if anyone's calling from it. You can't fix what you don't measure.

## How many leads should you actually expect?

This is the question behind the question, and I'll be straight with you: it depends enormously on your trade, your market, and how competitive your area is.

An emergency service — plumbing, HVAC, locksmith, water damage — tends to get more website leads because people search in a panic and call the first credible result. A business where people shop around for weeks, like a kitchen remodel, gets fewer inquiries but each one is worth more.

A salon in a small town might get most of its bookings from the website simply because there are only three salons and one of them has a site that works on a phone.

What I'd push back on is anyone promising you a specific number of leads per month before they've looked at your market. Nobody can know that up front.

The realistic framing: a decent site in a normal local market starts producing a trickle within a couple of months and builds from there as search rankings settle in. It's not a switch. It's more like seed that takes a season.

## What drives the cost up (and what's usually worth it)

Since you're going to ask — the things that push a website project more expensive are usually:

- Lots of individual service pages (more pages means more writing, but it's also usually where the leads come from)
- Online booking or scheduling that talks to your calendar
- Custom photography — expensive, but real photos of your crew outperform stock every single time
- E-commerce or payment handling
- Multi-location setups

What's usually included and shouldn't cost extra: mobile responsiveness, basic page speed, a contact form, your business info structured so Google reads it correctly. If someone's charging you separately for "mobile optimization" in this decade, that's a flag.

What's usually worth paying for even on a tight budget: good photos of your actual work, and a few real service pages instead of one catch-all "Services" page.

## How to tell if your current site is generating leads

Do this today, takes twenty minutes:

1. Search for your main service plus your town in an incognito window. Are you on page one? Anywhere?
2. Open your site on your phone. Time it. Over three seconds and you have a problem.
3. Can you tap the phone number? Does it dial?
4. Ask the last five customers how they found you. If nobody says "found you on Google," that's your answer.
5. Check if you have call tracking or form notifications set up at all. A shocking number of sites have forms that email an address nobody checks anymore.

That last one is more common than you'd think. I've found businesses sitting on months of unread inquiries because the form was pointed at an old email.

## The bottom line

A website generates leads the way a work truck generates revenue — only if you drive it somewhere. Sitting in the yard, it's just an expense.

The good news is that the fixes aren't mysterious. Get found, load fast, make it obvious what you do and where, and make contact effortless. Most local markets are still full of competitors doing none of that, which means the bar is lower than you'd expect.
