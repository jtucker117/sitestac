## The short answer

Yes, websites still matter — arguably more than they did five years ago. AI search tools like ChatGPT, Google's AI Overviews, and Perplexity don't invent answers about your business out of thin air. They read websites, directories, and review sites, then summarize what they find. If your business doesn't have a site with clear, current information on it, the AI has nothing to pull from and it recommends the plumber down the road instead.

The thing that's changed isn't whether you need a website. It's what the website has to do. For twenty years the job was "look professional and rank on Google." Now the job is "be the clearest, most machine-readable source of truth about your business on the internet." Those two jobs overlap a lot, but not completely.

Let's get into it.

## Where AI actually gets its answers about local businesses

There's a misconception floating around that AI models already "know" everything and just answer from memory. That's mostly wrong for local business questions.

When someone asks ChatGPT "who does emergency boiler repair in Hamilton," the model doesn't have a list of Hamilton boiler guys baked into its brain. It runs a live search, grabs a handful of pages, and summarizes them. Same with Google's AI Overviews — that summary at the top of the results page is assembled from pages Google already has indexed.

So the sources are:

- **Your own website.** The single source the AI treats as authoritative about what you do, where you do it, and how to reach you.
- **Your Google Business Profile.** Hours, location, reviews, photos, service categories.
- **Directories and listings.** Yelp, Angi, Houzz, HomeStars, Thumbtack, chamber of commerce pages, trade association member lists.
- **Review sites and social posts.** What customers said about you, in their words.
- **News, blogs, and local press.** Anywhere else your name shows up in a sentence.

Notice that only one of those is fully under your control. Your website is the only place where you decide exactly what gets said. Everything else is someone else's platform, someone else's formatting, someone else's rules. When the AI is cross-checking "is this business real and do they do what they claim," the website is the anchor.

No website means you're entirely at the mercy of a Yelp listing you haven't logged into since 2019.

## What actually changed about search

Here's the honest version of what's different, without the doom.

**Fewer clicks on informational questions.** If someone googles "how long does a water heater last," they're going to read the AI summary and move on. They were never going to hire you from that search anyway — they were curious. That traffic was mostly vanity traffic.

**More qualified clicks on buying questions.** When someone asks "best roofers near me for a cedar shake replacement," the AI gives a short list and the person clicks through to check them out. That click is worth ten of the old ones, because the AI already pre-screened them and they're deeper into deciding.

**Being mentioned in the summary matters more than ranking #4.** Position four on page one used to get you a trickle. Now, if the AI summary names three businesses and you're not one of them, a lot of people never scroll. Being *in the answer* is the new game.

**Zero-click doesn't mean zero-value.** If an AI Overview says "Smith Electrical in Dayton handles panel upgrades and offers same-day estimates," and the customer calls you directly from your Google listing — you got the job without the website click. But you only got mentioned because the website existed and said those things clearly.

That last point is the one owners miss. Your website can generate work it never gets credit for in analytics. Traffic going flat while the phone keeps ringing isn't a failure. It's the new normal.

## Why "I'll just use Facebook / Instagram / my Google listing" doesn't cut it

I hear this a lot from guys with full schedules. Fair enough — if the phone's ringing, it's ringing. But here's where social-only falls down when AI is doing the recommending.

**Social platforms are hard for AI to read.** A lot of Facebook and Instagram content sits behind logins, loads with JavaScript, or lives inside images. If your service list is a graphic you designed in Canva, the AI can't read it. It's a picture. Same with your prices, hours, and service area if they're in a story or a pinned post.

**You don't own the format.** A Facebook page is built for posts, not for "here's exactly what we do in each of the six towns we serve." There's nowhere good to put a detailed page about septic inspections or kitchen remodel timelines.

**A Google Business Profile is a summary, not a source.** It's maybe 200 words of description and a category. Great for the map pack. Not enough for an AI deciding whether you're the right fit for a specialty job.

**Platforms change the rules whenever they feel like it.** Reach changes, formats change, accounts get locked. Your domain doesn't.

A restaurant that posts its menu as a photo on Instagram is invisible when someone asks an AI "where can I get gluten-free pasta in town tonight." The same menu as actual text on a website page gets found.

## What makes a website AI-friendly

This isn't a different discipline from good SEO — it's the same stuff, done with more care about clarity. AI systems are basically trying to answer one question: *can I trust what this page says, and is it specific enough to be useful?*

### Say things plainly, in text

The single biggest fix for most small business sites. If it matters, it needs to be in text on a page, not in a logo, a banner image, or a PDF.

Bad: a hero image with "24/7 EMERGENCY SERVICE" baked into the picture.

Good: a line of text that says "We answer emergency calls 24 hours a day across Guelph, Kitchener, and Cambridge."

### Answer the actual questions people ask

AI pulls answers. So give it answers. Not "we pride ourselves on quality workmanship" — that's nothing. Instead:

- How long does a typical bathroom reno take?
- Do you need a permit for a deck in this county?
- What's the difference between a repair and a full replacement, and how do you decide?
- Do you charge for estimates?
- How far out are you booking right now?

Write those as real sections with real headings. An AI scanning your page for "does this contractor charge for quotes" will find a heading that says exactly that and lift the answer.

### Be specific about where you work

"Serving the greater metro area" is useless to a machine. List the towns. List the neighborhoods. If you drive to five counties, say which five. Local AI results lean heavily on geographic specificity because the whole point of the query is *near me*.

### Be specific about what you don't do

Counterintuitive but it works. A roofer who writes "we do asphalt shingle and metal roofing; we don't do flat commercial roofs" is giving the AI a clean signal. You'll get fewer bad leads and better matches.

### Keep your details identical everywhere

Your business name, address, and phone number should read exactly the same on your website, Google profile, Yelp, and every directory. Not "St." on one and "Street" on another. AI systems cross-reference these to confirm you're a real, single business. Mismatches create doubt, and doubt means you get left out of the answer.

### Use structured data

This is the one bit of technical plumbing worth caring about. Structured data (schema markup) is invisible code that spells out, in a format machines read instantly: this is a plumbing business, here's the phone number, here are the hours, here's the service area, here's the review rating. It removes guesswork.

You don't need to understand it — your web person does. But ask whether your site has it. Most cheap template sites don't.

### Show real proof

Photos of your actual jobs, with captions describing what the work was. Reviews on the page with the customer's town. Licenses and certifications listed with their numbers. Years in business. Team photos.

AI systems weight trust signals heavily, partly because the companies building them are terrified of recommending a scam. A site with real evidence of a real business gets recommended. A site that could've been generated in ten minutes doesn't.

### Load fast and work on a phone

If a crawler times out, your page doesn't get read. If the page needs heavy scripting to show its content, some crawlers won't see it. Fast, simple, text-first pages win.

## The content that earns AI mentions

If you want to show up in those summaries, write the stuff the summaries are made of.

**Service pages, one per service.** Not one page called "Services" with a bulleted list of eleven things. A real page for drain cleaning, a real page for sewer line replacement, a real page for water heater install. Each one explaining what's involved, what it costs more or less depending on, how long it takes, what the customer needs to do to prepare.

**Location pages that aren't junk.** A page for each town you genuinely serve, with something actually specific on it — the permit office, the common housing stock, the typical issues in that area. Older homes in a particular neighborhood with knob-and-tube wiring. Clay soil that wrecks foundations. Hard water that kills water heaters early. That's content no competitor can copy and an AI can use.

**Honest answers about cost.** You don't have to publish a price list. But explain what drives the number up and down. "A straightforward panel swap is the low end. Add a service upgrade from the pole, or a panel in a finished basement that needs drywall repair, and it climbs." Customers search this constantly, and AI tools love pages that give a real framework instead of "call for a quote."

**Project write-ups.** A short story per job: the problem, what you found, what you did, how it turned out. Photos. This is the easiest content for a trades business to produce and the most convincing, because nobody can fake it.

**A real FAQ page.** Collect the questions you actually get on the phone. Write them down. Answer them in two or three sentences each. This format maps almost perfectly onto how AI assembles answers.

## What to stop worrying about

A few things owners get anxious about that don't deserve the energy.

**Keyword density and all that old SEO trickery.** Stuffing "plumber Hamilton" into every sentence does nothing now except make your site read badly. Language models understand meaning, not repetition.

**Traffic numbers dropping while calls hold steady.** Already covered, but worth repeating. Measure the phone, the form fills, and the booked jobs. Not sessions.

**Blogging constantly.** Twelve thin posts a year about "5 Tips for Spring Maintenance" won't move anything. Three genuinely useful pages that answer real questions will outperform fifty generic ones.

**Whether AI will "steal" your content.** It's going to read your content and sometimes summarize it without a click. That's the deal now. The alternative — having nothing for it to read — is strictly worse.

## What this looks like in practice

Two HVAC companies in the same city.

Company A has a five-year-old site. Home page with a stock photo of a furnace, a services page with eight bullet points, a contact form. No town names beyond the city itself. Phone number is in a header image. No reviews on the site. Last updated when they changed their logo.

Company B has a page for furnace repair, one for AC install, one for ductless mini-splits, one for duct cleaning. Each explains the process, the typical timeline, and what makes a job more or less involved. Eight location pages for the surrounding towns, each mentioning the local housing stock and the common problems there. An FAQ with twenty real questions. Thirty job write-ups with photos. License number on every page. Reviews pulled in with the customer's first name and town.

Someone asks ChatGPT: "My 20-year-old furnace is short cycling, who should I call in Ancaster and what's it likely to cost?"

The AI needs a page that mentions short cycling, mentions Ancaster, and talks about repair-versus-replace economics. Company B has all three in one place. Company A has none of them. Guess who gets named.

That's the whole thing. It's not magic and it's not a trick. The business that wrote more useful, specific, honest information about its own trade gets recommended more often.

## How to tell if your site is pulling its weight

Quick self-audit you can do this afternoon:

1. Open your site on your phone. Time how long it takes to load. Over three or four seconds, you've got a problem.
2. Can you select your phone number as text, or is it part of an image?
3. Count your service pages. One per service, or one page for everything?
4. Search your own site for the names of the towns you serve. Do they appear?
5. Ask ChatGPT and Google directly: "best [your trade] in [your town]." Are you named? What does it say about you? Is it right?
6. Check your Google Business Profile against your website. Same name, same phone, same hours?
7. When was the last page added or updated? If you can't remember, that's the answer.

Most owners find three or four clear gaps in fifteen minutes. None of them require a rebuild — most are a few hours of writing and a bit of cleanup.

## The bottom line

AI didn't make websites obsolete. It made bad websites more obsolete and good websites more valuable.

The brochure site that just sits there and looks nice was always a bit of a waste. Now it's actively costing you, because it gives the AI nothing to work with while your competitor's site gives it everything.

The fix isn't complicated. Write clearly. Be specific. Answer the questions you get asked on the phone every week. Keep your details consistent across the internet. Show proof that you're real and good at what you do.

That's the same advice that worked in 2010. The machines just hold you to it more strictly now.
