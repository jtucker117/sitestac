## The short answer

A small business website in Houston usually lands somewhere between a simple one-page setup and a full multi-page build with service-area pages, and the price follows the size of your footprint more than anything else. A single-location salon inside the Loop costs less to put online than an HVAC company chasing calls from Katy to Baytown. The biggest cost drivers in this market are how many suburbs you serve, how many services you sell, and whether you need the whole thing in Spanish as well as English.

Everything else — the design, the photos, the fancy animation — moves the number far less than most owners expect.

## Why Houston quotes come in higher than other Texas cities

Houston isn't one market. It's a metro spread across Harris, Fort Bend, Montgomery and Brazoria counties, stitched together by 610, Beltway 8 and the Grand Parkway. A roofer in a mid-size city can rank for one town name and be done. A roofer here is competing for Cypress, Spring, Pearland, Sugar Land, The Woodlands and Humble — and those are all separate searches with separate competitors.

That's the real reason a Houston build costs more than the same build in a smaller market. Each area you actually want calls from needs its own page with its own content: the jobs you've done there, the drive time, the neighborhoods you cover. Ten of those pages is real work. Ten copies of the same paragraph with the city name swapped is not work, and Google treats it accordingly.

The second reason is competition depth. Houston has a lot of good trades. Foundation repair, storm restoration, generator installs, pest control, pool service, tree work, HVAC — these are crowded categories with established companies that have been collecting reviews for a decade. Getting a new site to compete there takes more content and more proof than it does in a thin market. Your builder is pricing against that reality, whether they explain it or not.

## The four things that actually move your price

**1. Service area coverage.** One location, one page. Twelve suburbs, twelve pages. This is usually the single biggest swing in a Houston quote.

**2. Number of services.** A lawn crew that mows and edges needs less than a company doing mowing, irrigation repair, drainage, tree trimming and sod. Every distinct service that people search for separately deserves its own page. "Drainage installation Houston" and "lawn care Houston" are not the same customer.

**3. Spanish.** A huge share of Houston customers search and hire in Spanish. A properly bilingual site — not a translate widget, actual Spanish pages that can rank — roughly doubles the content work. It's worth it for a lot of trades here, and it's dead weight for others. Decide on purpose, not by default.

**4. Anything that connects to another system.** Online booking for a salon or med spa, online ordering for a restaurant, a quote form that drops into your CRM, call tracking numbers. Each connection is more setup and more that can break. Useful, but they're the difference between a brochure and a tool.

## What should be in every quote, no matter who builds it

If these aren't included, you're not comparing the same thing:

- Fast loading on a phone over cell data, not office wifi
- Click-to-call in the header on every page
- A form that actually emails you and doesn't die silently
- Google Business Profile set up and connected properly
- Real service pages, not one page with a bulleted list of everything you do
- Reviews visible on the page, not just a link to Google
- Your service area written out clearly enough that a customer in Missouri City knows you'll come

## Where Houston owners overspend

Custom illustration and heavy animation. Nobody standing in a flooded garage in August is admiring your hero animation. They're looking for a phone number.

Stock photos of buildings that aren't in Houston. Free to swap out, but you paid someone to pick them. Fifteen photos off your own phone — your truck, your crew, a finished install — outperform a professional stock library for local trust.

Pages nobody reads. A long "Our Story" page, a blog set up and abandoned after two posts, a team page for a two-person shop. Money spent on pages that get no traffic is money that should have gone into a Katy or Pearland service page.

Rebuilding every two years because nobody can edit the thing. Ask, before you sign, how you change your phone number or add a service page yourself.

## Where they underspend

Content about the work. Foundation repair, flood restoration, drainage, insurance claims after a hail storm — these are jobs where the customer is scared and researching hard. A page that explains how the process works, what the inspection covers, and what the timeline looks like closes more work than any design choice. That writing takes time and it's the first thing cheap builds skip.

Mobile call handling. Most Houston service searches happen on a phone, often from a driveway or a parking lot. If your number takes more than one tap, you're paying for traffic and giving it away.

Speed. A site that takes six seconds to load on LTE loses people before they see it. Big uncompressed photos are usually the culprit and it's a cheap fix — if someone bothers.

## Ongoing cost is the part people forget

The build is one number. Then there's hosting, domain, SSL, plugin and platform updates, backups, and someone to call when a form stops sending. Some shops bundle it, some bill hourly, some hand you the keys and disappear. None of those is wrong, but you need to know which one you bought.

If the answer is "we'll get to it when we get to it," that's a cost too. A dead contact form during hurricane season or the first real cold snap of the year costs a service company more than a year of maintenance.

## How to compare two quotes that look different

Ask both builders the same three questions: how many pages, who writes the content, and what happens after launch. Most of the price gap shows up in the answers. A cheap quote with "you provide content" means you're writing twelve service-area pages yourself on a Sunday night.
