## The short version

Setting up a Google Business Profile takes about 20 minutes: go to google.com/business, claim or create your listing, pick the right primary category, add your service area or address, verify (usually by video or postcard), then fill in hours, services, and photos. The setup is easy. Getting it to actually bring in calls comes down to choosing the right category and keeping the profile active after day one.

Here's the whole thing, step by step, plus the parts most people get wrong.

## Before you start, gather this

Save yourself the back-and-forth. Have these ready:

- **Your exact business name** — the one on your truck, your invoices, and your sign. Not "Bob's Plumbing | 24/7 Emergency Plumber Denver."
- **A phone number that a human answers.** Not a tracking number you might switch next month.
- **Your address**, or a list of the towns you serve if you work out of a truck.
- **Your hours**, including whether you actually take emergency calls at 2am.
- **10–20 photos** of real work. Phone photos are fine.
- **Your website URL**, if you have one.

If you don't have a website yet, you can still set up the profile. But leaving that field blank hurts you — more on that later.

## Step 1: Check if a listing already exists

Search your business name on Google Maps first. Google auto-generates listings from phone directories, old permits, and customer submissions all the time. If one already exists, you claim it instead of creating a duplicate.

Duplicates are a real headache. Two listings for the same shop split your reviews and confuse Google about which one to show. If you find one you didn't make, click **Claim this business** and go through verification.

If nothing shows up, go to google.com/business and start fresh.

## Step 2: Business name — type it exactly

This is the rule people break most. Google's guidelines say your name field is your real-world business name, full stop. No city, no keywords, no "Best Rated."

Stuffing keywords there used to work. Now it gets you suspended, or a competitor reports you and Google strips the whole listing. If your legal name genuinely includes a service word — "Miller Electric" — great, you get that for free. If it doesn't, don't fake it.

## Step 3: Pick your primary category carefully

This is the single biggest lever in the whole setup, and it takes 30 seconds.

Your primary category tells Google which searches you're eligible for. A shop listed as "Contractor" will not show up for "roof repair near me" the way a shop listed as "Roofing Contractor" will. Be as specific as the list allows.

A few real examples:

- A shop that mostly does drain clearing should be **Drainage Service** or **Plumber**, not **Handyman**.
- A salon that's 80% color work should be **Hair Salon**, not **Beauty Salon**.
- A guy who installs and services HVAC should be **HVAC Contractor**, with **Air Conditioning Repair Service** as a secondary.

Add secondary categories for real services you offer, but don't pile on ten of them. Three or four accurate ones beat a dozen loose ones. Your primary category carries the most weight by a wide margin.

Not sure what to pick? Look at the top three competitors already ranking for the search you want. Their categories are visible in the page source, but easier: whatever their listing says under the business name is usually the primary.

## Step 4: Address vs. service area

Two setups, and picking the wrong one causes problems.

**Storefront** — customers come to you. Restaurant, salon, tire shop, dental office. Show your address publicly.

**Service area business** — you go to customers. Plumbers, electricians, mobile detailers, landscapers. Hide your address and list the towns or zip codes you serve.

If you run out of your house, hide the address. Google lets you, and you don't want your home address on Maps.

One thing to know: your service area doesn't override geography. Listing 40 towns doesn't make you rank in all 40. Proximity to the searcher still matters a lot, which is why [ranking on Google Maps](/blog/how-to-rank-on-google-maps/) is harder the further out you go from your actual location.

## Step 5: Verification

Google will ask you to prove the business is real. Depending on your category and location you'll get:

- **Video verification** — most common now. You record a walkthrough on your phone showing your signage, your tools or equipment, and something proving you manage the place (keys, a bill with your name, your point-of-sale screen). Takes five minutes.
- **Postcard** — mailed to your address with a code. Takes a week or two.
- **Phone or email** — rare, but fast when offered.

For video verification, the biggest failure reason is not showing proof of management. Film your branded truck, then your tools in it, then yourself unlocking the cab. Don't cut it short.

If verification fails, you can try again. It's annoying, not fatal.

## Step 6: Fill in everything else

Once you're verified, complete the profile. Google rewards complete listings, and more importantly, customers choose the business that answered their question.

**Services.** List them individually with short descriptions. "Water heater replacement," "sump pump installation," "gas line repair" — separate entries. These show up in search and feed Google's understanding of what you do.

**Hours.** Be honest. If you close at 4:30, say 4:30. Set special hours for holidays — nothing burns goodwill like someone driving over on Memorial Day to a locked door.

**Photos.** Real ones. Before-and-after shots, your crew, your truck, your storefront from the street so people can find it. Skip the stock photos; customers can tell instantly. Add a few new ones every month.

**Description.** A short paragraph on what you do and where. Write it for a person, not a search engine.

**Website link.** Point it at your actual site. A profile with a working website converts better and gives Google more to confirm you're legitimate. ## Step 7: Reviews and the part that never ends

Setup is one afternoon. The profile only works if you keep feeding it.

Ask for reviews. Grab your review link from the dashboard, text it to customers right after the job while they're still happy. Two or three a month, consistently, beats a burst of twenty and then nothing.

Reply to every review — good and bad. A calm, specific reply to a bad review does more for your reputation than the five-star ones above it.

Post updates occasionally. Seasonal promos, a note that you're booked out three weeks, a photo from a job. It signals the listing is alive.

And check your info every few months. Anyone can suggest an edit to your hours or address, and Google sometimes accepts them.

## Common mistakes worth avoiding

- Keyword-stuffing the business name
- Using a call-tracking number that changes
- Leaving the address visible when you work from home
- Picking a vague primary category
- Setting it up and never touching it again
- Creating a second listing because you forgot the login to the first
