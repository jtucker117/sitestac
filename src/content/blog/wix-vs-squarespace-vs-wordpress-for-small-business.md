## The short answer

For most small local businesses, Wix is the easiest to build yourself, Squarespace looks the best with the least effort, and WordPress gives you the most control but expects you to know what you're doing. If you're a one-truck plumber who wants a site up this month, Wix or Squarespace. If you're running a multi-location business, a big service area, or you need things like booking systems and custom quote forms wired together, WordPress.

That's the 30-second version. Here's the longer one, because the platform matters less than most people think — and the stuff nobody mentions matters more.

## What each one is actually good at

### Wix

Wix is drag-and-drop in the most literal sense. You grab a text box, you move it where you want it, it stays there. For an owner who has never built anything online, that's a real advantage — there's no layer of abstraction between what you click and what happens.

It also has the widest spread of built-in tools. Appointment booking, basic email marketing, online payments, a simple CRM — it's all in the box. A salon that needs clients to book a color appointment without calling can get that running in an afternoon.

The downside: that same freedom means it's easy to build something that looks fine on your laptop and falls apart on a phone. Wix has mobile editing, but it's a separate view, and plenty of DIY sites end up with overlapping text on an iPhone because nobody checked. Since more than half your traffic is on a phone — usually closer to three-quarters for trades — that's not a small problem.

### Squarespace

Squarespace is the opposite philosophy. You pick a template, you fill in the blanks, and the design mostly holds itself together. You can't drag a headline three inches to the left, which sounds limiting until you realize that's exactly why Squarespace sites rarely look amateur.

It's the strongest choice if your business sells on looks. Restaurants, photographers, salons, med spas, high-end remodelers. Big photos, clean type, it handles all of it without you fighting anything.

Where it gets thin is anything beyond a brochure site. Squarespace has fewer add-ons than Wix or WordPress, so if you need something specific — a quoting calculator, a service-area page builder, integration with your field service software — you're often out of luck or stuck with a clunky workaround.

### WordPress

WordPress runs a huge chunk of the web, and for good reason: there's almost nothing it can't do. Want a site that pulls in your Google reviews, generates a page for every town you service, syncs leads to your CRM, and loads in under two seconds? WordPress, no question.

But WordPress isn't a product you log into — it's software you install on hosting you buy, with a theme you choose and plugins you maintain. That's four moving parts, and you own all of them. Updates break things. Plugins conflict. Sites get hacked when nobody's watching.

I've seen a contractor's site go down for nine days because a plugin update conflicted with the theme and the guy who built it had moved on. That's the WordPress risk in one sentence. It's not fragile when it's maintained. It's very fragile when it isn't.

## The question that actually decides it

Forget features for a second. Ask yourself this: **who is going to update this site in eighteen months?**

If the answer is "me, in the evenings," go with Wix or Squarespace. The hosting, security, and updates are handled. You log in, change your hours, log out. That's worth more than any feature list.

If the answer is "nobody, honestly," that's the real warning sign — and it applies to every platform. A site nobody touches goes stale fast. Your prices change, you add a service, you hire two more crews, and the site still says what it said the day it launched.

If the answer is "someone I'm paying," then the platform is mostly their call, not yours. A good builder will pick what fits your business. ## What about Google rankings?

All three can rank. That's the honest answer, and anyone who tells you Wix "can't do SEO" is working off information that's a decade old.

What moves the needle is the same on every platform:

- **Page speed.** A slow site loses people before they read a word. WordPress can be the fastest or the slowest depending entirely on how it's built. Wix and Squarespace are consistently middle-of-the-road — never great, never terrible.
- **Real content about real services in real places.** A page titled "Services" ranks for nothing. A page titled "Emergency Drain Cleaning in Fayetteville" ranks for something.
- **Your Google Business Profile.** For local trades, this often drives more calls than the website itself. None of these platforms affect it.

Where WordPress genuinely pulls ahead is at scale. If you need forty location pages, each one targeted properly, WordPress makes that manageable. Building forty pages by hand in Squarespace is a miserable weekend.

## The cost conversation

Nobody can give you a straight number here, but you can understand what drives it.

Wix and Squarespace bill you monthly and that's mostly it. Predictable. The cost creeps up when you start stacking apps — booking, email, extra storage — and each one adds a little.

WordPress looks cheaper on paper because the software is free. Then you're paying for hosting, probably a premium theme, a few paid plugins, and either your own time or someone else's to keep it running. The software being free is the least relevant fact about it.

The thing that costs the most on any platform is rebuilding. Picking the wrong one, spending six months fighting it, and starting over — that's the expensive path, and it's the most common one.

## A simple way to choose

- **You're doing it yourself and you want booking built in** → Wix
- **You're doing it yourself and the site needs to look sharp** → Squarespace
- **Multiple locations, big service area, or custom workflows** → WordPress
- **You'd rather spend that time quoting jobs** → hire it out and let them choose

None of these is a wrong answer. The wrong answer is picking a platform because a friend of a friend swore by it, then discovering six weeks in that it won't do the one thing your business actually needs.
