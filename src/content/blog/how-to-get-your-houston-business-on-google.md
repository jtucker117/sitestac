## The short answer

To get your Houston business on Google, claim and verify a free Google Business Profile, pick the right primary category, set a service area you can realistically cover, and connect it to a website that lists the same name, phone number, and service areas. Then start collecting reviews steadily. Verification usually takes days to a couple of weeks; ranking in the map results takes longer and depends heavily on where in the metro you're trying to show up.

That's the whole recipe. The reason it's harder here than in most cities is Houston itself.

## Houston is not one local market — it's a dozen stacked on top of each other

When someone in Katy types "AC repair near me," Google isn't looking at Houston. It's looking at a few miles around that phone. Same search from Pearland, Cypress, Spring, or inside the Loop pulls a completely different set of companies.

The metro sprawls across Harris, Fort Bend, Montgomery, Brazoria, and Galveston counties. Driving from one side to the other can eat an hour and a half on a bad afternoon. Google knows that. It isn't going to show a Humble-based electrician to someone searching from Sugar Land when there are forty electricians closer.

So the single most important thing to understand before you start: **your one business pin can't dominate the whole metro.** You can win a chunk of it. Which chunk depends on where you're physically located and how much of a reputation you build.

That's different from a smaller city where one profile covers everybody. Here, you have to be deliberate.

## Step 1: Claim the profile, and be honest about your address

Go to Google Business Profile and search for your business name. If a listing already exists — Google auto-generates them from phone books and old data — claim it rather than making a second one. Duplicates fight each other and both end up buried.

Then the fork in the road: storefront or service area.

If customers come to you — a salon in the Heights, a taqueria in East End, a tire shop off Airline — you list your street address and it shows on the map. Straightforward.

If you go to customers — plumbers, roofers, foundation guys, lawn crews, mobile detailers — you set it up as a **service area business**. Your address gets hidden and you list the cities or ZIP codes you cover.

Houston-specific warning: do not rent a mailbox in a nicer suburb and pretend that's your shop. Google has gotten aggressive about verifying addresses, and a suspended profile is a genuine headache to get back. It's more common than you'd think in this market, because contractors want a Woodlands or Sugar Land pin. Verification often involves a video call where you're asked to show signage, equipment, and mail — a mailbox store won't survive it.

## Step 2: Get your service area right, not big

The instinct is to check every city from Conroe to Galveston. Resist it.

A huge service area doesn't help you rank further away — Google weighs your actual location for proximity regardless. What an oversized area does do is send you calls from 50 miles off that you'll either turn down or lose money driving to.

List what you actually serve and want. A Cypress-based HVAC company is better off owning Cypress, Jersey Village, Tomball, and northwest Harris County than being a ghost in twenty listings.

If you genuinely operate across the metro with crews staged in different areas, that's a case for separate physical locations — but each one needs a real staffed address. Not a warehouse you visit twice a month.

## Step 3: Your primary category is doing most of the work

Google lets you pick one primary category and several secondary ones. The primary is the heavy lifter.

"Plumber" and "Drainage service" pull different searches. "Roofing contractor" and "Roof repair service" aren't the same. If you make most of your money on foundation repair — and plenty of Houston contractors do, because the clay soil under most of this region moves with every wet-then-dry cycle — then "Foundation contractor" should be primary, not "General contractor."

Set the primary to your bread and butter. Add the rest as secondary. Don't stuff keywords into your business name either; "ABC Plumbing Houston Best Water Heater Repair" is a guideline violation and competitors report it constantly here. The market is competitive enough that people do watch each other's listings.

## Step 4: Reviews are the tiebreaker, and Houston raises the bar

In a market this size, you're not competing with three companies. In most Houston trades you're competing with dozens inside your own slice of the metro, and the top of the map pack usually has hundreds of reviews.

You don't have to match that overnight. What matters more than raw count is **steady flow**. Twelve reviews spread over a year beats forty that all landed in the same week — which looks bought, because it usually is.

Practical version: text the review link the same day the job wraps, while the customer still feels good about it. Don't wait until the invoice is paid a month later.

And reply to every review, including the bad ones. A calm, specific reply to a complaint does more for a homeowner reading it than five generic five-stars.

One bonus: reviews often mention the neighborhood on their own. "Came out to our house in Pearland after the storm" is exactly the kind of language that helps Google connect you to that area — and you didn't have to write it yourself.

## Step 5: Point it at a website that backs it up

A Business Profile with no website behind it hits a ceiling fast. The profile establishes that you exist. The site is where a homeowner decides whether to call you or the next company down.

Three things matter here more than design:

- **The same phone number and business name everywhere.** Profile, website footer, Facebook, old directory listings. Mismatches make Google less confident it's all the same business.
- **Real pages for the areas you serve.** Not one "Service Areas" page with a list of forty cities. A page that talks about what you actually do in Katy, another for Sugar Land, written like a person wrote it.
- **Loads fast on a phone on LTE.** Most of your calls come from someone standing in a hot garage or a flooded utility room. If it takes six seconds to load, they've already tapped the next result. ## Step 6: Photos, posts, and the Houston calendar

Upload real photos. Your truck, your crew, finished work, the storefront. Stock photos of a smiling stranger in a hard hat fool nobody, least of all a homeowner who's been burned before.

Then use the profile seasonally, because Houston demand is deeply seasonal:

- Late spring through September, AC and attic work is all anyone is searching for.
- Hurricane season runs June through November, and after any serious storm the searches turn to roof tarps, water extraction, tree removal, and generators.
- A hard freeze — rare, but it happens — turns burst pipes into a week of nonstop calls.

Post updates that match the moment. "Same-day AC diagnostics in northwest Harris County this week" is more useful in July than an evergreen paragraph about your values.

## Don't overlook Spanish-language searches

A lot of Houston customers search in Spanish. If someone on your crew handles calls in Spanish, say so — on the profile, on the site, in your posts. It's an easy edge that a surprising number of competitors skip entirely, and it turns a bilingual employee you already have into a lead source.

## What to expect on timing

Verification: days to a couple of weeks, sometimes with a video call. Showing up for searches with your business name: quickly. Showing up in the map pack for "[trade] near me" from a neighborhood you're not sitting in: months of consistent reviews, content, and activity.
